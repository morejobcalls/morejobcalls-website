The Problem

Why Most Patio Cover Marketing Fails

Patio cover contractors have a problem nobody else in home improvement has: your own suppliers compete with you for the customer. A homeowner searches for an Alumawood cover or a louvered roof, lands on the manufacturer's site, fills out the form, and that inquiry gets handed to several dealers in the same network. Same product. Same brochure. The only lever left is price.

Then there's the other squeeze. In your market, some guy with a trailer is bolting together $4K kit covers, and every homeowner who's seen his yard sign walks into your estimate anchored at a number you can't touch. You're quoting an engineered $35K louvered system to someone who thinks "patio cover" means four posts and a sheet of aluminum.

Here's the thing: demand isn't your problem. In Texas, Arizona, California and Florida, shade isn't a luxury purchase. A backyard without it is unusable five months a year. The problem is that the channels feeding you that demand either share it, resell it, or anchor it at kit prices before you ever shake a hand. The Math

What a Booked Patio Cover Appointment Actually Costs

Every article about marketing a patio cover business quotes a cost per lead. It's the wrong number, and it's why a lot of owners think they have a price problem when what they really have is a reach problem.

Cost per lead hides the three places the money disappears: how many of those homeowners pick up, how many book, and how many are standing in the driveway when you pull up. A $50 shared inquiry where two of ten answer and half of those book is a $500 appointment. A pricier inquiry that almost always turns into a sat appointment is cheaper, even though the invoice looks worse.

Seasonality

What Patio Cover Marketing Looks Like in the Off-Season

The heat angle sells itself in July. Nobody needs a strategy to sell shade to a man standing on a 140-degree slab. The question that decides your year is what happens in November.

Most patio cover contractors pull the ads when the weather turns, which is exactly when the auction gets cheap and the homeowner who has been thinking about it all summer finally has time to sit down with you. Demand does not disappear in October. The competition does.

Justin W.'s read after a winter on the system: "I've never experienced a January and a February that went this well. Never." Josiah W., who runs a deck company in a Montana market of about 90,000 people across eight cities, went from one job left on the books to booked through winter and into the next spring. Different climate, same mechanism: while everyone else was fighting over peak season, his calendar was already full.

Three things change in the off-season, and all three favor you. Cost per appointment tends to drop because fewer advertisers are bidding. You can promise an install date that means something, instead of telling a homeowner in July you can get to them in October. Follow-Up

Speed to Contact: a Shade Inquiry in July Is a Today Phone Call

More appointments can make your business worse if the back end is not ready. That's not a sales line, it's the most common way this goes wrong in week two.

A homeowner who taps a patio cover ad at noon in August is not running a six-month design process. They're hot, they're annoyed about it, and they'll talk to whoever calls back first and sounds like they build real structures. Chip P., a 33-year general contractor in California, was reaching about 2 of every 10 homeowners on the shared leads he bought. On an exclusive funnel, he was talking with at least 8 of 10. Same man, same phone.

So week one of the launch is spent on your side, not in the ad account: who answers the phone, what they say, how fast a real conversation happens, and what fires automatically when a homeowner doesn't respond or doesn't show. No-show recovery and a reactivation sequence for last summer's dead estimates usually find more money than any change to the targeting does.
