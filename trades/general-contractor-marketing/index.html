The Problem

Why General Contractor Leads Are So Hard to Sort

A general contractor can build almost anything, and that's exactly what makes most lead sources a mess. The same week brings a second-story addition, a bathroom gut, a sagging fence gate and somebody who wants a ceiling fan hung before their in-laws visit. Shared lead sites sell all of it to several companies at once, and you spend Saturday driving to estimates that were never your size.

Then there's the sales cycle. A kitchen or an addition can sit in "we're still thinking about it" for months while the homeowner collects bids, talks to the bank and argues about cabinets. Without steady follow-up, the job you bid in March gets signed by whoever called back in June.

The problem is rarely lead volume. It's that nobody screens for project type and budget before the appointment, and nobody follows up after it. Fix both, and the same ad dollars build a different business.

The System

How the General Contractor Appointment System Works

This isn't ad management. It's an appointment engine installed inside your business, in your own accounts, in six pieces:

  1. Agency Audit & Recovery Plan. We pull apart what you've spent so far: lead-site invoices, old ad accounts and the website that never brought in a project. Then we put in writing why it didn't work and what changes. You keep the document either way.
  2. 7-Day Launch Sprint. A qualification funnel built for general contracting. It asks about the project type, the rough budget, the timeline, whether plans or a designer are involved, home ownership and whether both decision-makers can attend. It's wired into your CRM with tracking from day one.
  3. The Neighborhood Authority Engine. Video of you walking finished projects and explaining how a remodel actually runs: the permits, the schedule and the surprises behind the drywall. It reaches homeowners while they're still planning, before they post a request that gets sold to four other contractors.
  4. Lead Handling War Room. A week-one session with your owner, estimator and office: speed-to-lead rules, budget conversations that don't scare people off and how to book so both spouses are at the table.
  5. Sales Calendar Max-Out Kit. Routing rules, text and email confirmations, no-show recovery and long-cycle follow-up that keeps open bids warm for months. Small-job inquiries get a polite no instead of a site visit.
  6. Pipeline Pulse Reporting. A weekly report with the three numbers that matter: new opportunities, appointments set and jobs sold. You check math, not vibes.

FAQ

General Contractor Marketing — Common Questions

Do you market to general contractors, or for them?

For them. We book homeowners who want a remodel, an addition or a custom build onto a general contractor's sales calendar. We don't sell products or services to contractors on behalf of suppliers.

What kinds of general contractor projects do you book?

Planned projects that need a sales appointment: kitchen and bath remodels, additions, whole-home renovations, ADUs and custom builds. We don't book handyman work or small repairs, and the funnel screens those out before they reach your calendar.
