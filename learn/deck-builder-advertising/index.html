Deck Builder Advertising: What Each Channel Costs Per Booked Appointment

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
Updated October 2026

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I sell one of the channels on this page. I will tell you where that biases me, and every number below comes from an account I actually run, with the builder named.

Key Takeaway

Deck builder advertising has one honest scoreboard: what you pay in ad spend for a homeowner who actually sat down with you, and what you pay for a signed job. In a Gulf Coast deck account I run, $5,109.15 of Meta spend over about 60 days produced 106 homeowner inquiries, 42 completed sales appointments and 12 signed jobs. That is about $48 an inquiry, about $122 a sat appointment, and about $426 of ad spend per signed job against jobs averaging just under $27,000.

Cost per click and cost per lead are where most deck ad reporting stops, and neither one connects to your bank account. Below: what each paid channel actually buys you, what a deck ad has to show, what changes in the shoulder months, and the three numbers that tell you whether your advertising is producing conversations or appointments.

Most pages about advertising for a deck company are written by people who have never had to explain a slow week to a builder with four guys on payroll. They tell you to run Google Ads and Facebook, post consistently, and watch your cost per lead. Then they stop exactly where the hard part starts.

Here is the part they stop before. An ad does not produce revenue. An ad produces a phone number. Everything between that phone number and a homeowner sitting at a kitchen table with your design in front of them is a separate build, and it is the half that decides whether your advertising made money. I run about 20 active builders, roughly $250K a month in managed ad spend, with $10M+ lifetime ad spend and $100M+ in client-booked jobs tracked across 100+ contractor accounts since 2024. The pattern is the same every time: the ad is the cheap part.

The marketplaces publish their own per-lead ranges, roughly $15 to $70 depending on job size, and the number looks reasonable right up until you count how many of those homeowners you actually reach. One was a tire kicker, and the other one he sent his son out to bid on a lot that turned out to be bare dirt. Buying shared leads, Chip was reaching about 2 of every 10 homeowners. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10. Same man, same phone, same trade. What a Deck Ad Has to Show

The creative decides more than the channel does. A boosted photo of a finished deck with your logo in the corner does not work anymore, and it has not for years. What works is the owner on camera, real finished work shot at the height a person standing in their own yard would actually see it, and one specific offer.

Design-first is the whole trick. The homeowner cannot picture the thing yet. They have a sloped yard, a door that opens onto nothing, and a vague idea involving a hot tub. They are not shopping for a contractor, they are stuck on a decision. An ad that hands them the decision, a free custom design and estimate, gets a response from people who would scroll past "call us for a quote" a hundred times.

What kills a deck ad, in the order I see it:

  • A discount instead of an offer. Ten percent off teaches the homeowner to negotiate before you have met. A design teaches them to want the project.
  • Stock photography. Homeowners can tell. It also means the deck in the picture is not one you can build in their climate with your crew.
  • No qualification. If the calendar sits in front of the application instead of behind it, you will spend your Saturdays on bare-dirt lots.
  • Asking for the sale in the ad. The ad only has to earn the next 30 seconds.

Qualification is the quiet one. On his first three weeks on a qualification funnel, Chip P. got 63 homeowner inquiries and exactly one of the 63 questioned him about his minimum. What Changes in the Shoulder Months

Deck advertising has a seasonality problem that most advertisers get exactly backwards. The decision and the build happen in different seasons. Homeowners decide on a deck in winter. The shovel moves in April. If your ads only run when the shovel moves, you missed the decision by three months.

So the shoulder months are not the time to cut the budget, they are the time to change the offer. Sell the spring slot, not the winter install. "Free custom design and estimate now, install slotted for spring" is something a homeowner can say yes to in January without thinking about frozen ground. And the attention is cheaper, for the dumbest possible reason: most of your competitors turn their ads off in November, which leaves the auction to whoever stayed in it.

Chip P. books work in November for six to eight months out while the rest of his market waits for spring. That is the whole advantage, and it is available to anybody willing to keep the lights on in the off-season. The month-by-month version, October through March, is in the winter playbook for deck builders.

One real constraint on the other side: in a hot month, working advertising will outrun your crews. Chris W., who runs a deck company in Gulf Shores, Alabama, hit 13 to 14 sales appointments a week on $100 a day and was booked out 10 weeks, and we paused the ads on purpose while he hired three more guys. Capacity, not demand, is what limits a deck company once the advertising works.

The Honest Verdict

If I were starting a deck company's advertising from zero tomorrow, in this order: paid social into a free custom design and estimate, with an application in front of the calendar and a real follow-up cadence behind it. Then retargeting, because it is cheap. Then a narrow search campaign once there is volume to learn from. Shared marketplaces only if there is a specific reason, and never as the base of the business.

And I would judge all of it on two numbers, every Monday: cost per completed sales appointment, and ad spend per signed job. If an advertising report you are paying for cannot produce those two, it is not measuring your advertising. It is measuring its own activity. Common Questions

How much should a deck builder spend on advertising?

Work it backwards from one job instead of picking a percentage. In the Gulf Coast account above, about $426 of ad spend produced a signed job averaging just under $27,000. At $100 a day that account produced 13 to 14 sales appointments a week, which is more than most two-crew companies can physically run. So the real ceiling on your budget is not money, it is how many estimates you can sit in a week and how much work your crews can absorb. Start at a number you can spend for 60 straight days without flinching, hold it steady, and judge it on cost per sat appointment rather than on cost per click.

What is the best advertising channel for a deck builder?

Paid social into a free custom design and estimate offer, for most deck companies most of the year. A deck is a want-purchase, not an emergency, so only a small slice of your market is searching for a builder in any given month. Search ads rent that small slice and everyone in your county bids on it. Paid social reaches the homeowner who has been thinking about the backyard for two years and has never typed anything into Google. Search is usually the right second channel, not the first, and the shared-lead marketplaces are last because the homeowner you paid for also belongs to three of your competitors.

What does a deck lead cost compared to a booked appointment?

They are different numbers and only one of them predicts revenue. In a 60-day Gulf Coast deck account, $5,109.15 of ad spend produced 106 homeowner inquiries at about $48 each, and 42 of those became completed sales appointments at about $122 each. The marketplaces publish their own per-lead ranges, roughly $15 to $70 depending on job size, and then hand the same homeowner to about four contractors. Justin W., a deck and patio builder in San Antonio, was paying roughly $400 a lead for about 15 leads a month before he switched, and inside 14 days he was getting 30 a week at under $30. Ask for cost per sat appointment and ad spend per signed job. Anybody quoting only cost per lead is quoting the cheap half of the problem.

Should a deck builder advertise in winter?

Yes, and it is usually the cheapest attention you will buy all year, because most of your competitors switch their ads off in November. The decision and the build happen in different seasons: homeowners decide on a deck in winter and the shovel moves in spring. If your ads only run when the shovel moves, you missed the decision by three months. Do not cut the budget in the shoulder months. Change the offer instead and sell the spring slot rather than a winter install. Chip P., a 33-year veteran general contractor in California, books work in November for six to eight months out while most of his competition waits for spring.

Do Google Ads work for deck builders?

They work, and they are capped by something no agency controls: how many people in your county are searching for a deck this month. Published deck PPC guides put clicks in the $4 to $12 range, and in a trade where nothing is broken and nobody is in a hurry, every builder in the market is bidding on the same few hundred searches. Run it narrow when you run it: the handful of terms where somebody is clearly shopping for a builder, a landing page about that exact thing, and an aggressive negative keyword list to keep out the enormous DIY audience that will happily spend your budget. Leave Performance Max alone until you have conversion volume to feed it.

What does a deck ad actually have to show?

The owner on camera, real finished work shot at the height a person standing in their own yard would see it, and one specific offer, usually a free custom design and estimate. A boosted photo of a finished deck with the company name on it does not work anymore. Neither does a discount, which trains the homeowner to negotiate before you have met. The ad only has to earn the next 30 seconds, so send it to an application that qualifies on budget, timeline and decision maker, and put the calendar behind that application rather than in front of it.

How long before deck ads produce appointments?

Days, not months, if the follow-up layer is built before the ads turn on. Josh T., a Minnesota deck builder, went on 10 sales appointments in his first week, four of them in a single day. Ricardo C., a Colorado concrete and deck contractor, got enough appointments in his first seven days to close $105,000 in new jobs on under $1,000 of ad spend. That speed is a property of the follow-up, not the ad. Six months is an honest timeline for search. It is not an honest timeline for paid social, and anybody selling you paid ads who asks for a six-month patience window is asking you to fund their learning curve.
