Speed to Lead for Deck Builders: Why Deck Leads Go Cold

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
Updated October 2026
Key Takeaway

Speed to lead for deck builders means calling a new inquiry inside five minutes when you can and inside the hour as a floor you never break. A deck is not a burst pipe, so you are not losing an emergency in minute six. You are losing your place in a comparison the homeowner runs over the next week, and the builder who shows up first gets to set the terms of it.

Speed is the second half of the job, though. It multiplies whatever the inquiry already was. Call a tapped-out instant form back in ninety seconds and you reach the wrong person faster. Get the source right first, then run a real cadence: automatic text in seconds, human call in minutes, three attempts on day one, light touches out to thirty days.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I have an obvious interest in how this conversation goes, and you should read it knowing that. I am going to give you the uncomfortable half anyway, because the usual advice about speed to lead for deck builders is half right in a way that costs people money.

Here is what you will find if you search this topic. A dozen pages telling you that 78% of buyers go with whoever answers first, that responding in five minutes makes you 21 times more likely to qualify a lead, and that you should therefore buy their software. Almost none of them tell you where those numbers come from, and none of them are written about a deck company.

What Speed to Lead for Deck Builders Actually Means

Speed to lead is the clock that starts when a homeowner hits submit and stops when a human from your company actually talks to them. Not when your CRM sends an email. Not when you see the notification. When somebody says hello.

That distinction matters because most builders measure the wrong end of it. They will tell me "we get back to everybody same day," which is usually true and usually means the first live conversation happened six hours after the homeowner was thinking about decks. By then a homeowner who filled out one form in the evening has filled out two or three more before bed, and you are not the first call. You are the third opinion.

About the famous statistics: the five-minute and 21x figures almost all trace back to one study of business-to-business web leads published in Harvard Business Review in 2011. It is a real study and it is directionally right. It is also fifteen years old and it is not about a homeowner weighing a $40,000 to $100,000 backyard against a kitchen remodel. I would rather tell you that than quote a number at you like it came from decks.

A deck is a considered purchase, so the decay curve looks different than it does for emergency trades. Nobody is standing in two inches of water. What you are losing as the clock runs is not an emergency, it is position in a comparison that is about to happen whether you are in it or not.

The Part Nobody Selling You Software Will Say

Speed is a multiplier, not a fix. It multiplies whatever the inquiry already was.

If the inquiry came from an instant form inside a social app, where the homeowner's name, email and phone auto-filled after one tap, then calling back in ninety seconds means you reach the wrong person faster. That is not a follow-up problem. That is a lead that was never a conversation. I wrote the long version of that argument in the breakdown of why contractor leads don't pick up the phone, and it is the piece to read first if your contact rate is bad no matter how fast you dial.

Chip P., a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California, was reaching about 2 of every 10 homeowners on the shared leads he was buying. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10. Same man, same phone, same trade. His dialing speed did not change. The thing on the other end of the phone did.

So the order of operations is: fix where the inquiry comes from, then fix how fast you answer it. Doing it in the other order is how builders end up with a very fast system for annoying strangers.

Two rules make that table work. First, every attempt stops the moment you have a real conversation, because the cadence exists to start a conversation, not to finish a checklist. Second, somebody owns it by name. A cadence that belongs to everybody belongs to nobody, and it dies in week three.

Who Makes the Call When You Are On a Ladder

This is the actual blocker for most one-to-five-crew deck companies, and no article about the five-minute rule ever addresses it. You cannot answer in five minutes if you are forty minutes into a joist layout.

So decide in advance, before the inquiry lands, who has free hands. On most small deck companies the honest answer is a spouse, an office person, a sales rep, or a setter you pay for. Any of those beats the owner deciding on a ladder whether this one is worth climbing down for, because that decision always resolves the same way and it always resolves wrong.

The automation layer should do the parts that do not need a person. An instant text so the homeowner knows a real company saw them. A self-booking calendar in that text so the ones who are ready book themselves at midnight without waiting on you. A reminder sequence so the booked ones show up. What the automation cannot do is scope a deck, read a hesitation, or decide whether this homeowner is a planner or a tire-kicker. That is the call, and the call still has to happen.

Booked Is Not Sat: The Appointment Nobody Counts

Here is the layer that almost all speed-to-lead advice skips entirely. You can win the race to the phone, book the appointment, and still get nothing, because the homeowner is not home when you pull up.

A booked appointment is a promise. A sat appointment is revenue. The distance between them is almost entirely reminders nobody sent. Confirm in writing the moment it is booked. Remind the day before. Remind the morning of. Make at least one of those touches come from a human who names the homeowner, the address and the project, because a human confirmation is a social contract and an automated one is a calendar notification.

This is why I measure cost per completed appointment instead of cost per lead. On Chris W.'s account, 106 inquiries became 42 completed appointments. Completed is the only word in that sentence that paid anybody. What to Track, and What to Ignore

Three numbers tell you whether your speed problem is real:

Median time to first live conversation. Median, not average, because one four-day straggler will hide a good week. If you cannot pull it from your CRM, write it on a whiteboard for two weeks. You will not like the number and that is the point.

Contact rate by source. What share of inquiries you ever reach, split by where they came from. If one source sits at 2 in 10 and another at 8 in 10, you do not have a follow-up problem on the second one, you have a lead-source problem on the first.

Booked-to-sat rate. The percentage of booked appointments that actually happen. Anything you do upstream is wasted if this one is leaking, and it is the cheapest number on the list to fix.

What to ignore: cost per lead on its own, and any dashboard that reports "responded" when a template went out. Neither one corresponds to a homeowner who talked to you. The Bottom Line

Speed to lead is real and it is oversold at the same time. Calling in five minutes instead of five hours will win you jobs you are currently losing, and it will not do a thing about a list of people who never meant to talk to you. Get the source right, then get fast, then get persistent, then confirm the appointment so it actually happens.

The feast-or-famine cycle in this trade is not usually a lead-volume problem. It is a pile of inquiries that got answered on Tuesday. Pick up the phone on Saturday.

Common Questions

How fast should a deck builder call back a new inquiry?

Inside five minutes when you can, inside the hour as the floor you never break. The five-minute target is not magic, it is just the window where the homeowner is still sitting there with the phone in their hand and your name is still the one they are thinking about. The hour matters more than most builders believe, because a homeowner who filled out one form in the evening has usually filled out two or three more by bedtime. You do not need a call center to hit it. You need one person whose phone buzzes and whose job it is to answer, and a rule that says an inquiry outranks almost anything that is not a customer standing in front of you.

Does the five-minute rule really apply to deck projects?

Partly, and the honest answer is more useful than the slogan. Most of the five-minute statistics you see quoted in contractor marketing trace back to one 2011 study of business-to-business web leads, not to a homeowner thinking about a forty to one hundred thousand dollar backyard. A deck is a considered purchase. Nobody is standing in two inches of water. What that changes is the shape of the decay, not the direction of it. You are not losing a burst-pipe emergency in minute six. You are losing position in a comparison the homeowner is running over the next week, and the first builder to show up gets to set the terms of that comparison.

What should I do if I cannot answer the phone on a job site?

Decide in advance who answers, and make it somebody whose hands are free. On most small deck companies it is the owner's spouse, an office person, a sales rep, or a setter you pay for. What does not work is you deciding in the moment, on a ladder, whether this one is worth climbing down for. Build the answer into the system instead: an automatic text the second the inquiry lands so the homeowner knows a real person saw it, a self-booking calendar link in that text so the serious ones book themselves without waiting on you, and a human call inside the hour for everyone who did not. The automation buys you the hour. It does not replace the call.

How many times should I follow up with a deck inquiry?

Far more than once, and far more than feels polite. Most builders call once, get voicemail, and quietly write the inquiry off as a bad lead. Three attempts across the first day, spread across different times of day and across both call and text, will reach a large share of the people one call missed. After that, keep touching lightly out to about thirty days, because a homeowner who went quiet in week one is often just waiting on a spouse, a bonus, or a decision about whether to do the kitchen first. Quiet is not no. Quiet is almost always timing.

Should I text or call a deck lead first?

Both, in that order, seconds apart. The text should be automatic and go out the moment the inquiry lands, because it is the cheapest way to tell a homeowner that a real company saw their request. The call should be human and come within minutes, because text alone rarely gets you to a scoped project and a booked slot. Lead with the text so you are already in their phone when you ring, and so the number you are calling from is one they have a reason to recognize.

Will responding faster fix bad leads?

No, and this is the part the rest of the internet will not tell you. If the inquiry came from an instant form that auto-filled somebody's name and number after one tap, calling back in ninety seconds just means you reach the wrong person faster. Speed multiplies whatever the inquiry already was. Chip P., a deck builder in Loomis, CA, was reaching about two of every ten homeowners on the shared leads he was buying. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least eight of ten. Same man, same phone, same trade. Nothing about his dialing changed.

What is a good contact rate on deck inquiries?

It depends entirely on where the inquiry came from, which is why a single benchmark is misleading. On shared marketplace leads, reaching two of ten is common and that is mostly a function of the lead, not the follow-up. On a qualification funnel where the homeowner typed their own information, answered questions about project type, timeline and budget, and chose to ask for a call, eight of ten is a reasonable bar. If you are well under the number your source should produce, look at speed and persistence first and at the ad after that.

How do I stop booked appointments from no-showing?

Treat the confirmation as part of the sale rather than as admin. A booked appointment is a promise, and a sat appointment is revenue, and the gap between them is almost always reminders nobody sent. Confirm in writing the moment it is booked, remind the day before, remind again the morning of, and make sure one of those touches comes from a human who names the homeowner, the address and the project. On one Gulf Coast deck account, 106 homeowner inquiries produced 42 completed appointments over about sixty days. Completed is the only word in that sentence that paid anybody.
