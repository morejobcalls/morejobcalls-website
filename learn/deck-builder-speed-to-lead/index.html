Deck Builder Speed to Lead: The Exact Follow-Up Sequence From First Text to Sat Appointment

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Deck builder speed to lead means an automated text within seconds of the inquiry, a calendar link instead of a "we'll call you" promise, and a live call attempt inside five minutes for anyone who didn't book. After that, the sequence matters more than any single call: three attempts day one, daily through day four, reminders the day before and morning of, a no-show recovery text at the curb, and a 30/60/90-day reactivation lane for estimates that stall.

The reason it's worth building is contact rate. On a $50 lead, reaching 2 of 10 makes it a $500 appointment; reaching 8 of 10 makes it a $125 appointment. Speed is the biggest lever on that gap you control after the form.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. This page is the deck builder speed to lead playbook: how fast to get to a homeowner who just asked for a deck estimate, and the exact order of everything that happens after that first touch. It's about speed and sequence, not about why inquiries go quiet in the first place. That side, the auto-filled instant forms and the accidental taps, is covered in why contractor leads don't answer, and if your problem is homeowners who swear they never filled anything out, read that one first. This one assumes the inquiry was real and asks what you do in the next five minutes and the next ninety days.

Every number here is either arithmetic you can check or a figure from a named deck builder's account. There are no industry response-time statistics on this page, on purpose. You don't need a study to know that a homeowner who asked about a deck at 4pm Thursday is thinking about it at 4:01 and about something else by Saturday.

Why a Deck Inquiry Goes Cold Faster Than a Repair Call

A leaking water heater gets fixed this week no matter who calls back. A deck doesn't. It's a want purchase that lives on a to-do list for 30 to 90 days, and the homeowner's attention is only fully on it for the few minutes around the moment they raised their hand. They were on their phone, probably scrolling, probably thinking about summer. They asked. Then the next post loaded and the deck slid back down the list.

Two things follow. The company that reaches them inside that window sets the frame for the whole project: what it should cost, what it's built from, how long it takes. Everyone who calls Friday is compared to that company. And most deck buyers ask more than one builder, so there is always a Friday caller. Speed decides which one you are.

The clearest picture of what slow costs a deck company is Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes in Gulf Shores, Alabama. On $100 a day of ads he ran 13 to 14 sales appointments a week and booked $320,000 in deck jobs in about two months. He also had 40 or 50 homeowners who asked for a deck estimate and never got a call back, because the two people running the company were on job sites and in driveways from the moment the ads turned on. His words on the recorded call: "We lost so many things through Facebook, just losing track of people we didn't follow up with." Those weren't bad inquiries. They were the inquiries that arrived when nobody was set up to answer, and now they think E&C doesn't return calls.

Deck Builder Speed to Lead: The Response-Time Math

The reason to care about minutes is contact rate, and contact rate is the number that decides what a lead really costs. Here's the arithmetic the rest of this site uses, with round numbers to show the mechanism.

Take a $50 lead. If you reach 2 of every 10, you didn't pay $50 per lead, you paid $250 per conversation. If half of those conversations book, that's a $500 appointment. Now take the same $50 lead and reach 8 of 10. That's a $62.50 conversation and, at the same booking rate, a $125 appointment. Same ad, same invoice, four times the calendar. That gap is what what a deck lead really costs once you count the no-shows is about, and it's why per-lead price tells you so little.

Chip Paynter, a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California, lived both sides of it. Buying leads, he was reaching about 2 of every 10. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10. Qualification did part of that, because people who fill out a real survey meant it. Speed did the rest, because the system texted them the moment they finished and offered a calendar instead of a callback. Nothing on his per-lead invoice moved. His contact rate did.

The instant text, and the calendar instead of a form

The moment a homeowner finishes the survey, a calendar appears so they can pick a time while intent is at its highest, and a text and email go out with your name, the project they asked about, and the same link. No pitch, no price, under 40 words. The calendar is the part most deck companies skip and the part that does the most work: a form that says "we'll be in touch" hands the timing back to you, and you're on a ladder. Josh Throldahl of Throldahl Construction went on 10 sales appointments in his first week on this setup, four of them in one day, and described it as "It was just like boom — lead, confirmation, meeting." Derek Lopez of Highline Outdoor Living had two appointments booked within six hours of launch. Neither happens through a form and a callback.

The call attempts

Homeowners who qualified but didn't book get a live call inside five minutes, a text behind the voicemail with the link again, two more attempts that day, one a day through day four, then two the following week. Roughly ten touches in ten days, which sounds aggressive until you remember they asked you to come quote a deck. Change the message as you go, and make the last one an easy out ("want me to leave this until spring?"), because that gets more replies than any touch before it.

Reminders, no-shows and reactivation

A booked deck appointment is not a sat one. Confirmation fires at booking with the address, time, who's coming, your photo, and a request that both decision-makers be there, because most deck no-shows and most "let me talk to my wife" endings are the same problem. Then reminders the day before and the morning of, with retargeting ads running in between so they know who's pulling into the driveway. Ricardo Cervantes, a concrete and deck contractor in Colorado, described it from the inside: "Every two days I see messages go out: 'Remember that you have an appointment with Ricky this day.' So the follow-up is automatic. I've had a lot of good compliments about that, our follow-up is top-of-the-line compared to other contractors." He closed $105,000 in new jobs in his first seven days on less than $1,000 of ad spend.

A no-show gets a text from the curb, one call, a rebooking link the same day and one more attempt the next morning, then a date 30 days out rather than a delete. Every stalled estimate goes into the same lane: day 30, something tied to their actual project; day 60, a season or schedule reason to move; day 90, a plain question about whether the deck is still on the list for next year; then quarterly. Deck buyers sit in a 30-to-90-day window and a lot of them decide in month three. If you stopped at touch two, someone else is there when they do.

Tooling: What Actually Runs This for a Deck Company

The failure mode in every deck company I take over isn't that they don't know to follow up. It's that follow-up lives in the owner's head, and the owner is on a job site. None of the stack is exotic.

A CRM with automations. Every row in the sequence table is a trigger and an action: "survey submitted" sends the text, "no booking after five minutes" creates a call task, "appointment booked" schedules the reminders, "appointment time plus 15 minutes" sends the curb text. Any modern contractor CRM does this. The software isn't the point; the point is that the sequence fires whether you remember or not. One setup note: texting compliance registration happens up front, because automated texts legally require it, and a company that skips it will have its messages filtered before the homeowner sees them.

A calendar instead of a form. Replace "request a quote" with "pick a time," put real slots on it including a couple of evenings when both spouses are home, and show it on the thank-you screen, in the first text, and in every follow-up touch. The homeowner should never have to ask when you're available.

An AI setter for the first ten minutes and the hours nobody is awake. Set up well, it answers the text thread in seconds, confirms the project and timeline, and offers calendar times at 11 PM when the owner is asleep. Set up badly, it pretends to be you, quotes a price, and tries to answer a question about ledger flashing. The rule I use: it can qualify and schedule, it hands off to a human the moment a homeowner asks something a builder should answer, and it always speaks in the owner's name and voice. Used that way it raises contact rate. Used as a way to never talk to people, it lowers close rate, and close rate is the more expensive number on a deck.

That layer is most of what we actually build. The ads get the credit, but the ads were rarely the broken thing in the accounts I've taken over; the gap between the click and the backyard was. How those pieces fit together for a deck company is on the deck builder marketing page.

What This Looks Like for a Two-Man Deck Crew

Most deck companies are an owner, a lead carpenter, and a truck, so here is the version that survives a Tuesday where you're both setting posts. The first rule: nobody swinging a hammer should be the only person responsible for the five-minute call. Chris Walters is the case study. Two people, $100 a day, appointments booked inside the first 24 hours, 13 to 14 a week by the second month, and 40 or 50 homeowners who never heard back because the two people were the crew. He got booked out ten weeks and paused the ads to hire, which was the right call, but the 40 or 50 were the cost of turning on demand before deciding who answers.

Brian Wallace at Bend Fence & Deck is the other side. Before the system he was running two to four appointments a day, only in the window after the annual home show, and only because he was doing all the follow-up himself. With his daughter Jessica running point on the CRM and the automation working each homeowner until the appointment was locked in, he sustained five to seven appointments a day and sent 60 estimates in six weeks, closing about 25. Homeowners were telling him, "I got the text this morning — I've been waiting for you." Same builder, same town. The sequence ran without him.

How to Measure Your Own Speed to Lead

Four numbers, all available from CRM timestamps if the sequence runs through it. Minutes to first touch, as a median, because one inquiry that sat over a weekend hides a week of good behaviour. Contact rate: of ten inquiries, how many became a real conversation. Booking rate: how many conversations became a time on the calendar. Show rate: how many bookings you actually sat. If your CRM can't tell you minutes to first touch, that's the first fix, because you can't shorten what you can't see. Then look at when inquiries arrive; in most deck accounts a real share land in the evening and on weekends, exactly when a working owner isn't answering, and that one report usually settles whether the automation is worth building.

The Short Version

Automate the first touch so it fires in seconds. Put a calendar where the form was. Call inside five minutes if they didn't book, and keep calling on a schedule the CRM builds, not one you remember. Confirm, remind, and get both decision-makers to the table. Recover no-shows from the curb. Reactivate at 30, 60 and 90 days. And decide, before the ads go on, whose phone rings between 7am and 7pm, because the accounts that skip that decision are the ones with 40 or 50 homeowners who never got a call back.

FAQ

Deck Builder Speed to Lead: Common Questions

How fast should a contractor respond to a lead?

Inside five minutes, and inside five seconds for the automated part. The first text should fire the moment the homeowner submits, and a live call attempt should follow within the first five minutes if they didn't book a time themselves. A homeowner who asks for a deck estimate at 4pm on a Thursday is thinking about their backyard at 4:01 and about something else by the weekend, and the first company that reaches them sets the frame for everyone who calls after. If nobody in your company can pick up between 7am and 7pm, solve that before you spend on demand.

What is speed to lead for contractors?

Speed to lead is the time between a homeowner raising their hand (a form, a survey, a call, a message) and the first real response from your company. For contractors it's measured in minutes, not business days, because home-improvement inquiries are made on impulse from a phone and cool off fast. The number to track is minutes to first touch, and the number it drives is contact rate: how many of ten inquiries you actually get a conversation with. Everything else on the calendar sits downstream of those two.

Why do deck leads go cold so fast?

Because a deck is a want, not an emergency. Nobody has to build a deck this week, so the homeowner's attention is only on it for the few minutes around the moment they asked. They're usually scrolling, they usually ask more than one company, and the project sits inside a 30-to-90-day consideration window where any distraction pushes it back down the list. Speed is your only lever on that first window. The reasons the inquiry might have been weak to begin with, like instant forms that auto-fill, are a separate problem covered in why contractor leads don't answer.

What should the first text to a deck lead say?

Three things, in under 40 words: who you are by name and company, that you got their deck request, and a link to pick a time on your calendar. Something like: "Hi Sarah, it's Mike at Ridge Decks. Got your request for a composite deck estimate. Grab a time that works and I'll come take a look: [link]. Or reply here with any questions." No pitch, no price, no paragraph. The job of the first text is to get a reply or a booking while the homeowner is still looking at their phone.

How many times should a deck builder follow up with a lead?

For a qualified homeowner who hasn't booked, plan on three call attempts the first day, one a day for the next three days, and two spread across the following week, with a short text after every unanswered call. That's roughly ten touches across ten days, which sounds like a lot until you remember that the homeowner asked for the estimate. After that, move them into a 30/60/90-day reactivation lane rather than dropping them. Set it up as automation so it happens whether you remember or not; the builders I work with who do this by hand always stop around touch two.

Does an AI setter work for a deck company?

For the first ten minutes and for the hours nobody is awake, yes. An AI setter can answer the text thread in seconds, confirm the project and timeline, and offer calendar times at 11 PM when the owner is asleep. What it should not do is pretend to be you, quote a price, or answer a real building question about footings or ledger boards. Set it up to hand off to a human the moment a homeowner asks something a builder should answer, and keep the owner's name and voice in the messages. Used that way it raises contact rate; used as a replacement for ever talking to people, it lowers close rate.

What do I do when a deck appointment no-shows?

Text at the appointment time plus fifteen minutes ("I'm at the curb, did we miss each other?"), call once, then send a rebooking link the same day. Try once more the next day. If they rebook, they go back into the confirmation and reminder cadence; if they don't, they move to the 30-day reactivation touch instead of being written off. The bigger fix is upstream: confirm at booking, remind the day before and the morning of, and make sure both decision-makers know who is coming and when. Most deck no-shows are one spouse booking without checking with the other.

How does speed to lead change the cost of a deck lead?

It changes it more than the sticker price does. A $50 deck lead where you reach 2 of 10 people is a $250 conversation, and if half of those book it is a $500 appointment. The same $50 lead where you reach 8 of 10 is a $62.50 conversation and a $125 appointment at the same booking rate. Chip Paynter, a 33-year veteran contractor in Loomis, California, went from reaching about 2 of 10 on bought leads to talking with at least 8 of 10 on an exclusive qualification funnel. The qualification did part of that and speed did the rest, and nothing on the per-lead invoice tells you which side of that gap you're on.
