Speed to Lead for Contractors: Why the First Hour Decides the Job

Spencer Wright · Founder, MoreJobCalls.com
Published October 28, 2026
Quick Answer

Speed to lead for contractors is the time between a homeowner asking for a quote and your company actually talking with them. Aim for five minutes and never break one hour. In a Harvard Business Review study of 1.25 million leads, companies that tried within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead.

Fast isn't the finish line, though. The clock that pays you stops when the visit is booked and the homeowner is actually home when you knock.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company that books exclusive sales appointments onto the calendars of high-ticket home-service contractors. We've booked 20,000+ of them since 2024, so I've watched a lot of good jobs die in a notification tray.

Every contractor I've ever met answers leads fast. Ask them. They'll swear to it from a roof, while a quote request from 9:14 this morning sits unread on a phone riding in the truck cupholder.

I'm not judging. I once left my own mother on read for two days, and she taught me manners. Homeowners aren't your mother, though. They don't wait.

This guide covers roofing, windows, HVAC, remodeling and fence. Decks get their own guide, speed to lead for deck builders, because deck homeowners shop on a slower clock.

What Is Speed to Lead for Contractors?

Speed to lead for contractors is how long it takes your company to have a real two-way conversation with a homeowner after they ask for a quote. A notification doesn't count. An auto-text doesn't count either. The clock stops when a person from your company is talking with the homeowner.

I track two clocks, not one. The first runs from quote request to first conversation. The second runs from quote request to a booked appointment, and that's the one that tells you whether you'll eat this month.

Most owners only watch the first clock. That's how a company calls back in four minutes and still loses the job. They had a lovely chat, said "I'll get you on the schedule," and the homeowner booked a competitor on Thursday (then sent a very polite text about it, which somehow makes it worse).

What the Research Says About Contractor Response Time

The research points one way: the first hour is worth more than the rest of the week. Most speed-to-lead numbers you see online are recycled from a handful of studies, so here's what each one actually measured, with the source linked.

StudyWhat they measuredWhat they found
Harvard Business Review, 20111.25 million leads at 29 consumer and 13 business-to-business companiesTrying within an hour made a company nearly 7 times as likely to qualify the lead as waiting even an hour longer, and more than 60 times as likely as waiting 24 hours
Harvard Business Review, 2011Test leads sent to 2,241 US companies37% answered within an hour. 23% never answered at all. The average reply took 42 hours
InsideSales.com and MIT, 200715,000+ web leads and 100,000+ call attempts at six companiesThe odds of reaching a lead drop 100 times between a 5-minute call and a 30-minute call. The odds of qualifying drop 21 times
Valve+Meter, via Professional Remodeler, 2018Secret-shopper requests to 466 home services companiesOnly 60% responded within five days. No reply in the first 6 hours meant a 75% chance of no reply ever
Invoca home services report, 2026Calls to home services businesses, including doors and windows, HVAC and construction52% of callers reach a person. 55% of businesses don't ask the caller to book the job

Yes, the HBR study is from 2011, the year half the country was flinging Angry Birds at pigs on their lunch break. The MIT one is from 2007, which makes it older than some of the shingles you're tearing off. Neither is about contractors. The Valve+Meter and Invoca numbers are, and they say the same thing, only louder.

Read the red bar again. Nearly a quarter of companies never answered a lead somebody had paid to get. Somewhere an owner bought the click, got the form, and let it ripen in his inbox next to a 2019 invoice for gutter guards.

Here's the part that should cheer you up. If a quarter of the market never calls back and the average reply takes 42 hours, you don't need to be a genius to win. You need to be awake.

Why the First Hour Decides the Job in Every High-Ticket Trade

The first hour decides the job because a homeowner who asks for one quote usually asks for several, and the first company to have a real conversation gets to define the project. Everyone after that is quoting against the first company's version. How fast that window closes depends on the trade.

TradeWhat the homeowner is doing in hour oneHow long you really haveWhat the late company walks into
Roofing, active leakStanding under a drip holding a mixing bowlMinutesSomeone else already has a tarp on it
Roofing, old roofFinally asking after three years of "next spring"Hours, then the nerve fadesA homeowner who talked themselves back out of it
WindowsFilling out three or four quote forms in one sittingAbout an hourYou're quote number four, and the comparison is already set
HVAC replacementSweating in July or wearing a coat indoors in JanuaryMinutes to same dayThe first company that answered is already in the attic
RemodelingCollecting names for a kitchen or bath they've wanted for yearsA day or two, but first still sets the scopeThe first contractor's scope and budget are now the yardstick
FenceHas a new dog, a pool inspection, or a neighbor problemSame dayA crew that answered is already walking the property line

The time windows are what we see on the accounts we run, not a published study.

The pattern is the same in all five trades. Whoever talks to the homeowner first sets the terms: the scope, the budget range, and what "normal" sounds like. Everyone after that gets graded on somebody else's test.

Windows are the brutal one. Window shoppers fill out forms like they're entering a sweepstakes, then wait to see who's serious. Remodeling is the slow one, but slow isn't safe. The first remodeler in the kitchen gets to describe the dream, and everyone after gets to describe the price.

Phone calls leak the same way forms do. In Invoca's 2026 data, only about half of the people who call a home services business reach a person.

Where those homeowners come from matters as much as how fast you call them. Our page on exclusive home improvement leads covers the sources that send a homeowner to you alone.

How to Respond to Contractor Leads Faster When You're on a Roof

To respond to contractor leads faster, take the first response off the owner's phone. Send an automatic text the second a form comes in, give the homeowner a link to book a visit, and name one person whose hands are free to call within five minutes. The owner becomes the backup, not the plan.

Here's the problem with "just call faster." You're 30 feet up in a harness, holding a nail gun, and the phone is buzzing in your back pocket against a 10/12 pitch. If you answer at all, the homeowner hears wind, a compressor, and a man who sounds like he's being chased by geese.

So the real question isn't how fast you can call. It's who calls when you can't.

Who answersGood atWatch out for
The owner, between tasksKnows the work coldLadders, crawl spaces, and the drive to the supply house
A spouse or office managerFree hands, knows the calendarNeeds a short script and permission to book
A dedicated appointment setterSpeed and persistenceHas to pass along scope notes so the visit isn't a cold start
An answering serviceNights and weekendsTakes messages, rarely books
Automatic text plus a booking linkInstant, 24/7, never on a roofCan't read hesitation or scope a job

Notice what's missing from that table: "I'll get to it at lunch." Lunch is when the crew holds its daily investigation into who took the good tape measure.

One thing works in your favor. In the InsideSales.com and MIT data, 4 to 6pm was the best time of day to reach a lead, 114% better than calling just before lunch. That's when most crews knock off. Your best calling hour is the drive home, so put a callback block there.

A Follow-Up Cadence That Works From a Ladder

A follow-up cadence that works from a ladder splits the work. Automation handles the first seconds and the reminders, and a human makes a few well-timed calls. Pack the calls into the first day, switch to texts after that, and stop chasing a quiet homeowner after about a month.

The switch after day one isn't a hunch. The MIT team found that after 20 hours, every extra dial actually hurt the odds of qualifying the lead. Past that point a call stops being follow-up and starts being the number they block.

5 to 20 minutesIf no answer: a "sorry I missed you" textSecond call, same number

After bookingConfirmation, day-before and morning-of remindersA personal text or call the day before

Two rules keep it alive. Every automated message stops the instant the homeowner replies, because nothing kills trust like a robot asking "still interested?" an hour after they booked. And one named person owns the human column, so it never belongs to "whoever sees it," which in my experience is a guy named Nobody.

Contractor Leads Not Answering? Check These Five Things First

When contractor leads aren't answering, the cause is usually one of five things. The lead was shared, your number is a stranger, you called once, you called at a bad hour, or the form asked for almost nothing. Each one has a fix, and most of them cost nothing.

Why they don't answerThe fix
The lead was shared with three other companiesSpeed still helps, but buy exclusive. Being the first of four strangers to interrupt her dinner wins a very small trophy
Your number is a strangerText first, then call from the same number seconds later
You called onceThree calls on day one finds the people who were making dinner
You called at the worst hourIn the MIT data, 1 to 2pm was the worst hour to qualify a lead. 8 to 9am was 164% better
The form asked for nothingAsk about the project, the timeline and the budget. One-tap forms get one-tap intent

If you've fixed all five and the phone still rings out, the problem is upstream of the phone. The full diagnosis is in why contractor leads don't answer, including the questions that filter out the button-tappers before you ever dial.

Booked vs Sat: How to Reduce the Time Between Quote Request and Booking

To reduce the time between a quote request and a booking, book the appointment on the first call and offer a specific time in the next few days. Don't promise to "get back to them." Then protect the booking, because a booked appointment only pays when it's sat: the homeowner is home and you're at the kitchen table.

StageWhere it leaksThe fix
Quote request to first conversationSlow answer, or no answerInstant text, call inside five minutes
First conversation to bookedNobody asks for the appointmentBook on the call. Offer two specific times
Booked to satForgotten, spouse not home, cold feetConfirm, remind the day before and the morning of, both decision makers present
Sat to signedQuote emailed laterPresent the offer at the appointment

Invoca's 2026 home services report found that 55% of home services businesses don't ask leads to buy or book the job. Sit with that one. More than half the companies that win the race to the phone forget to ask the one question the race was for.

I've heard it on recorded calls. Great conversation, homeowner ready, and the contractor says "let me check my calendar and text you." The text goes out Tuesday, and the homeowner booked someone else Monday night.

That last row is a hill I'll defend. A $30,000 roof or a $60,000 kitchen gets decided at the table, by the people paying for it, with you there to answer questions. A PDF can't answer questions. It just sits in an inbox looking expensive.

What to Automate in Your Follow-Up, and What to Keep Human

Automate everything that's the same for every homeowner: the instant reply, the booking link, the missed-call text-back, the reminders, and the alerts to whoever is on call. Keep human anything that changes with the person: the first real conversation, scoping the project, the spouse question, and the price.

Common Questions

What is a good speed to lead for contractors?

Under five minutes during working hours, and never over one hour. In a Harvard Business Review study of 1.25 million leads, companies that tried within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead.

How do contractors respond to leads fast while they're on a job?

Take the first response off the owner's phone. An automatic text with a booking link goes out instantly, and one named person with free hands calls within five minutes.

How many times should a contractor follow up with a new lead?

Three calls on the first day, then texts. In the InsideSales.com and MIT study, every extra dial after 20 hours hurt the odds of qualifying the lead, so switch to a weekly text out to about 30 days.

Why aren't my contractor leads answering the phone?

Usually the lead was shared, your number is a stranger, you called once, you called at a bad hour, or the form asked for too little. Text first, then call from the same number.

What is the difference between a booked and a sat appointment?

A booked appointment is a time on your calendar. A sat appointment actually happened, with the decision makers at the table. Confirm every booking and remind the day before and the morning of.
