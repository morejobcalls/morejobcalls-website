14 Questions to Ask a Deck Marketing Agency (and What the Answers Should Sound Like)

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Ask every agency the same 14 questions in the same order, grouped by what they test: exclusivity, ownership, what gets measured, the guarantee, the funnel and follow-up, creative, reporting, and the contract. Score each answer 0, 1 or 2 against the weak-versus-real column, and don't hear a price until Group 8.

Three of the fourteen are disqualifiers on their own: an agency that can't define "exclusive" in contract language, runs your ad spend through its own bank account, or won't put what it said into writing is out, whatever the total.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I'm one of the people you'd be interviewing; read this as the script I'd want run on me and on everyone else who pitches you. The questions to ask a deck marketing agency below are grouped by what each one actually tests, and every question comes with what a weak answer sounds like next to what a real answer sounds like, because on a sales call the wording is the evidence.

The builders who get burned rarely picked a bad agency out of a lineup of good ones. Justin Wylie at All Pro Decks & Patios in San Antonio went through six agencies before he found one that stuck. Brian Wallace at Bend Fence & Deck paid an ads company and got 15 dead leads in two months. Billy Stewart at Trinity Decks spent $250,000 on leads over his career. None of them were careless. They had no way to tell a confident pitch from a true one.

This page is the call itself: what to say, in what order, and how to hear the answer. Grading the agency on paper, red flags included, is the job of the nine-point scorecard for the best marketing agency for deck builders.

How to Interview a Marketing Agency: Run It Like a Hire, Not a Demo

Most contractors let the agency run the call, and by the time they get a question in they've absorbed forty minutes of the rep's framing. Flip it. This is how to interview a marketing agency the way you'd interview a lead carpenter.

  • Send the list ahead. A specialist is relieved, because the answers are already in their contract. A generalist gets nervous. Both reactions are information.
  • Same questions, same order, every agency. Three agencies is enough to see the spread. Ten is procrastination.
  • Write the answers down close to verbatim. "We'd never do that" and "here's the clause" are not the same answer, and you'll forget which one you heard by Friday.
  • Score each answer 0, 1 or 2 against the columns below, on the call.
  • Keep price for the end. Groups 1 through 7 decide whether the number is worth hearing.

The same fourteen work as questions to ask a marketing agency for contractors in any trade. Swap "deck" for fence, roof or pool and nothing changes, because they test how the agency is built, not what it knows about decking.

The 14 Questions to Ask a Deck Marketing Agency, Grouped by What They Test

The left column is what you say. The middle is what you'll hear from an agency that hasn't built the thing you're paying for. The right is a real answer, and notice that almost every one includes a number or points at a document.

Group 1: Exclusivity

Every agency says "exclusive." The word costs nothing. These two find out whether it's a clause or a mood.

Ask thisWhat a weak answer sounds likeWhat a real answer sounds like
Q1. Define "exclusive" as it will read in my contract. Can a homeowner from my ad ever reach another contractor through you?"All of our leads are 100% exclusive." No definition, no territory, no clause. Or: "We would never do that to a client.""You only. One deck builder per market, the market is defined in the agreement as these counties, and no other client of ours sees a homeowner from your ads. Here's the clause."
Q2. Who else do you run within an hour of me, in any trade, and what happens when a second deck builder in my metro wants to sign?"We can't discuss other clients." Or "we manage the overlap carefully." Overlap the agency manages is overlap you'll never see.Names the trades nearby, confirms none are deck or outdoor living, and says the second builder gets turned down or wait-listed for as long as you're a client, in writing.

Group 2: Ownership

If the account isn't yours, nothing else on this page matters, because the day you leave you start from zero.

Q3. Whose card does the ad spend run on, and does a single dollar of my ad budget ever pass through your bank account?"We bundle media and management into one monthly number to keep it simple." They hold the account; you're renting your own customer data."Your card, straight to Meta or Google. We never hold spend. Not listing the assets tells you the assets aren't yours.A list, without hesitating: ad account and pixel (already yours), business page, landing page, creative files, a CRM export of every homeowner, and the automations or a written copy, within a stated number of days.

Group 3: What Gets Measured

Whatever unit the agency counts is the unit you'll pay for. Make them define it before they promise you a number of it.

Ask thisWhat a weak answer sounds likeWhat a real answer sounds like
Q5. Define "appointment" exactly the way you'll count it toward whatever you're promising me."Anyone who books a time." Or worse, "any qualified lead." When the unit is vague, the agency decides later what counted."A homeowner who answered the qualifying questions, picked a date and time on your calendar and received a confirmation. No-shows count as opportunities; sat appointments are tracked separately, and you see both."
Q6. What's the show rate across your deck accounts right now, how do you measure it, and what do you change when it drops?"Really strong. Our clients love the quality." No number, no method.A range that swings by market and season, how it's tracked (the owner marks sat or no-show in the CRM), and the levers: reminder timing, qualifying questions, the offer in the ad.

Group 4: The Guarantee

The four remedy types, and which one costs the seller anything, are in who actually guarantees appointments for contractors. These two assume you know which type you're offered and test whether it's real.

Ask thisWhat a weak answer sounds likeWhat a real answer sounds like
Q7. Has anyone ever collected on your guarantee? Tell me what happened."We've never had to pay out," said with pride and no detail. Either it isn't true, or the conditions make a payout impossible.An honest account of a miss and how it was settled, or a plain walk-through of what triggers the remedy, who decides, how fast it's paid, and a link to the public terms.
Q8. Read me every condition I have to meet to keep the guarantee alive, then tell me which one your clients most often break."Just stay engaged with the process and you'll be fine." A condition you can't check is one the agency interprets after the fact.A finite list: minimum daily budget without gaps, calling new appointments within 24 hours, attending onboarding, no unauthorised campaign changes. Then: "Response time. Owners get busy in July and stop calling back the same day."

Group 5: The Funnel and Follow-Up

Almost nobody asks about this group, and it's where the money is. Chip Paynter, a 33-year veteran general contractor in California, was reaching about 2 of every 10 leads he bought. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10. The difference was everything between the click and the backyard.

Q9. A homeowner submits at 9pm on a Saturday. What happens in the next 60 seconds, the next hour and the next seven days, and who does each step?"You'll get an email notification with their contact details." The end of the agency's job and the start of yours, and why most bought records go cold before Monday.A text and email inside a minute, qualifying questions, a calendar link or a call from a booking team, confirmation, reminders the day before and the hour before, and a named no-show recovery sequence. Every step has an owner.
Q10. Where does the ad send people: a form, a phone number or a calendar? Why?"Instant forms give the lowest cost per lead." True, and it's exactly the problem: cheap records nobody can reach."A short qualifying application into your calendar. It raises cost per inquiry and lowers cost per sat appointment, because the homeowner picked the time and answered the budget question before you drove out."

Group 7: Reporting

Don't ask what they report. Ask to see it.

Ask thisWhat a weak answer sounds likeWhat a real answer sounds like
Q12. Send me a real report you delivered to a deck client last week, name blacked out if you need to.A dashboard screenshot of reach, impressions, clicks and cost per lead. Nothing on it connects to a signed deck. Group 8: The Contract

Only now do you talk about money and terms. Everything above decides whether the price is even worth hearing.

What to Do With the Answers

  • 24 to 28. Worth a second call. Before you sign, every answer you scored a 2 goes into the contract as a clause, in the words they used. If the answers were real, that takes them an afternoon.
  • 16 to 23. A genuine agency with gaps. Every question under a 2 becomes a written term or a reason you walk. The usual gaps are Groups 5 and 7: decent ads, a name handed over, follow-up and reporting left to you. That's the cheap half of the job.
  • Under 16. A generalist with a deck page, whatever they call themselves. Move on. A low price on a shared record was never the cheap part.

Compare Q5 across agencies before you compare price: if one counts "anyone who books a time" and another counts "qualified, confirmed, on your calendar," their per-appointment numbers aren't the same product. And be honest about Q8. If you can't call a homeowner back within 24 hours in July, the guarantee is void by week two, and the constraint to fix first is inside your own company.

Then run the script again at the 90-day mark. Does the weekly report look like the one they showed you? Is the show rate a number you've seen, or a feeling? The answers on the call were a promise. The 90-day answers are the product.

FAQ

Interviewing a Deck Marketing Agency: Common Questions

What are the most important questions to ask a deck marketing agency?

Three of the fourteen decide most of it. Ask them to define "exclusive" as it will read in your contract, ask whose card the ad spend runs on and whether any of it passes through the agency's bank account, and ask them to define "appointment" exactly the way they'll count it toward whatever they're promising you. An agency that answers those three plainly, with clauses to point at, is usually straight on the other eleven. An agency that hedges on any of them is telling you what the other eleven answers would have been.

How do I interview a marketing agency for my contracting business?

Run it like a job interview, not a sales call. Send the questions ahead so nobody is ambushed, ask every agency the same fourteen in the same order, write the answers down close to verbatim, and score each one 0, 1 or 2 before you compare price. Keep price for the last group, because once a number is on the table the conversation stops being about what you're buying. Three agencies is enough to see the spread; ten is procrastination.

Should I send the questions to the agency before the call?

Yes. A real specialist is relieved to get them, because the questions are the ones they've built their business around, and the answers are already in their contract. A generalist gets nervous, because the questions expose that the deck page on their site was written by the same person who wrote the roofing page. Either reaction is information, and you get it before you've spent an hour on a call.

What if the agency answers every question well but won't put it in the contract?

Then you don't have those answers. Verbal on a sales call is worth nothing in month five when the salesperson has moved on. Ask for an addendum covering exclusivity as a defined territory, ownership of the ad account and data, the definition of an appointment, the guarantee remedy and its void conditions, and the notice period. If they won't add a paragraph they've already said out loud, the paragraph was never true.

What is the difference between a lead and an exclusive sales appointment?

A lead is a homeowner's contact details, often sold to several contractors at once, that you then have to reach. An exclusive sales appointment is a homeowner who answered qualifying questions, picked a date and time on one contractor's calendar and received a confirmation, with nobody else getting the same record. The difference shows up in contact rate: Chip Paynter, a 33-year veteran general contractor in California, was reaching about 2 of every 10 leads he bought, and on an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10.

Do these questions work for a fence, roofing or pool company too?

Yes. Swap the word "deck" for your trade and every one of the fourteen still holds, because they test the agency's structure, not its knowledge of decking. The stakes scale with your ticket. A $60,000 pool or a $45,000 deck can afford a much higher cost per sat appointment than a $400 repair, so the questions about the funnel, follow-up and show rate matter more the bigger the project you sell.
