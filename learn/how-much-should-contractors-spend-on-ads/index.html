How Much Should a Contractor Spend on Ads? The Real Math

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
April 27, 2026 · Updated October 2026
Key Takeaway

"I can't afford to advertise" is almost never a budget statement. It is a trust statement. If someone credibly offered to hand you back fifteen times what you put in, you would take that trade every time, at any size. What you are really deciding is whether you believe the return will show up.

There is no universal right number, which is why we set it on a strategy call against your market, service area and crew capacity. The same dollar buys qualified appointments on a properly built system and tire kickers on a broken one. The budget is not what determines which one you get.

Most contractors hear a five-figure monthly ad budget and file it under expense. Money out, risk, something to avoid until business is better.

Watch what happens when you change one word. It is not an expense, it is a purchase of capacity, in the same category as a truck or a crew member. A truck is money out that becomes a tool. A hire is money out that becomes capacity. Ad spend, when the system behind it works, is money out that becomes predictable revenue.

The hesitation is rational, though, and worth naming: most contractors asking this question have already been burned. $20,000, $30,000, sometimes $50,000 handed to agencies that delivered shared leads and spam calls. So the brain does not hear "investment," it hears "here we go again."

What a Working Ad Budget Should Actually Produce

Here is the math I run with clients. Read it as ratios rather than a price tag, because the right number for you depends on your market, your service area and how many appointments your crew can absorb. It only holds if three things are true: appointments are exclusive, there is real qualification before anyone reaches the calendar, and the advertising builds authority rather than shouting offers.

Those leads are not accidental clicks. They answered project type, timeline, budget and location, then chose a time on your calendar. That is a different species from a shared lead, and it is why the close rate behaves differently.

Why the Same Budget Produces Opposite Results

Two contractors spend the identical $10,000 and end up in different universes, because one is running a system and the other is running what I would call hope-based advertising.

Hope-based looks like this: run some ads, use instant lead forms because they are easy, collect a pile of cheap opt-ins. The problem is that making it effortless to opt in teaches the platform to find people who opt into everything. You get volume and no intent, which is the most expensive kind of cheap.

Mike H., a family deck business in Minnesota, had been stuck at the same revenue for five years and had burned $27,000 with an SEO agency whose answer was always to give it more time. Twelve months produced about a dozen appointments. He told me he used to check whether his phone was working. After switching to an exclusive system he closed more than $45,000 of new work inside the first two weeks. The budget was never the variable.

Justin W., a deck and patio builder in San Antonio, was paying about $400 per lead through a national agency and getting roughly six leads a month. Rebuilt properly, his cost per lead fell below $30. The full breakdown of that change is in from $400 to $30 per lead in 14 days.

How Much Should a Contractor Spend on Marketing Overall?

Published contractor benchmarks put total marketing at about 3% to 10% of revenue, and where you land depends on how you get your work. In the 2018 Remodeling 550, the biggest full-service remodelers averaged roughly 3% of revenue on marketing, while replacement contractors averaged roughly 10%. Across large companies in every industry, Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey put the average marketing budget at 7.7% of company revenue.

The gap between 3% and 10% isn't about who's braver. It's about who's selling to strangers. In that same survey, 87% of the full-service firms named word-of-mouth as a top-three marketing activity, so referrals were doing a lot of their selling for free.

Ad spend is one line inside the marketing number, not the whole number. The rest is the website, the software, the photos and video, the yard signs and whoever runs it all. If your new appointments mostly come from ads, I'd expect the ad line to be the biggest piece, because the ads are doing the job referrals do for the 3% crowd.

Percent of revenue is a starting line, not a target. Chris W., a deck builder on the Alabama Gulf Coast, put $5,109.15 of Meta ad spend in over about 60 days and booked $320,000 of deck work, which is ad spend of under 2% of what it booked. I'd rather you set the ad line backwards from one job: what you can afford to spend to sign one, times the jobs your crews can actually build.

The Question to Ask Instead

Not "can I afford $10,000 a month." Ask: what does another year at this revenue cost me, while competitors book the homeowners who were going to call me eventually?

Waiting is not neutral. If the system would have returned even a fraction of the numbers above, every month of delay has a price tag, it just never shows up on a bank statement.

Before You Increase a Budget

Raising spend on a broken system multiplies the mess. Work through these first:

  1. Are leads exclusive to you, or sold to several contractors at once?
  2. Does anything qualify budget and scope before someone reaches your calendar?
  3. Can you trace a closed job back to the ad that produced it?
  4. Do homeowners see you explain your work before the appointment, or meet you cold?
  5. Do you know your cost per closed job, not just cost per lead?

If four of those five are shaky, fix them before adding budget. If they are solid, scaling is arithmetic rather than a gamble: when $10,000 reliably returns a multiple, $20,000 usually returns a similar multiple until you hit capacity, which becomes the real constraint.

The Honest Summary

Contractors stuck at one or two million tend to think in what ads cost. The ones at five and beyond think in what ads return. That is close to the whole difference, and it is a mental model, not a budget.

Run your own numbers before you take mine. If your system cannot trace a closed job back to a source, the first investment is not more ad spend. It is the tracking that tells you whether any of this is working.

How Much to Spend on Marketing: Common Questions

How much should a contractor spend on marketing?

Published contractor benchmarks put total marketing at about 3% to 10% of revenue, with ad spend as one line inside it. In the 2018 Remodeling 550, full-service remodelers averaged about 3% of revenue and replacement contractors averaged about 10%. Contractors who live on referrals tend to sit near the low end, and contractors who sell to strangers through ads sit near the high end. Use that range as a check, then set the real number backwards from what you can afford to spend to sign one job.

How much of a marketing budget should go to ads?

In Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey, companies put about 31% of their marketing budget into paid media, or roughly 2.4% of revenue (Marketing Brew). For a contractor whose new appointments mostly come from ads, that share usually runs higher, because the ads are doing the selling that referrals do for other contractors. The budget's only right if it returns a multiple you can repeat, so track cost per signed job, not just cost per lead.
