How to Get More Deck Referrals, and the Ceiling Nobody Mentions

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
October 23, 2026 · Updated October 2026

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So when a builder asks me how to get more deck referrals, I answer it in two parts, and the second part is the one nobody writes about. Yes, there are asks that work, and most deck companies make none of them. There is also a hard ceiling on the whole channel, and it is arithmetic, not effort.

Key Takeaway

You get more deck referrals by asking at the right moment, giving the homeowner something to hand over, and following up twice. Most builders do none of the three and call referrals "word of mouth," as if it happens on its own. The asks are easy and worth doing this week.

The part worth understanding before you build a plan around it: referral volume is a function of how many decks you finished, not how hard you ask. Double your asking and you might go from half a referral per job to one. Double your referrals and you still have to have doubled your jobs first. That is why referrals cannot be the engine. They are the exhaust.

What a Deck Referral Is Actually Worth

More than anything else you can buy. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

A referred homeowner shows up having already decided you are the person. They don't make you compete on price the same way, they don't ghost you after the measurement, and they sign faster. Everybody in this trade knows that, which is why "we're 90% referral" gets said with pride on every podcast in the industry.

I have watched it from the other side too. I connected one client, Brian W., a fence and deck builder in Bend, Oregon, with one of my deck builder clients in Texas. That client called Brian back from his Trex trip in the Bahamas to tell him: "This guy's legit, and if you hire him, you better be ready, because you're gonna get a lot of leads." Brian's read on it: if a busy contractor interrupts his vacation to give a referral, that referral means something. That is the whole power of the channel in one sentence, and it is why I would never tell a deck builder to stop working it.

Here is the problem with building a company on it. A referral is a reward for work you already finished. You cannot schedule it, you cannot scale it on demand, and in a seasonal trade it arrives at the worst possible time of year. Chip P., a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California, had sent out five or six deck bids in the first ten months of 2025 running mostly on referrals. Five or six bids. In ten months. He closes around 90% of the jobs he actually gets in front of, so his business was never a selling problem. It was a volume problem, and referrals were not going to fix it.

Why Referral Volume Is Capped by Your Job Count

This is the part the referral articles leave out, and it is the reason a referral-run deck company plateaus at a number and sits there for years.

Referrals are a percentage of finished jobs. So your referral volume next year is set by your job volume this year, times whatever rate your asking produces. Getting better at asking moves the rate. It does not move the job count, and the job count is the bigger of the two numbers.

Run the arithmetic on your own business. Every rate below, the referrals per job and the close rate both, is illustrative rather than a published benchmark, and I am not aware of anyone who has measured either properly across this trade. Put your own numbers in the same shape and the conclusion holds anyway.

At 12 decks a year it gets you 8, and 8 does not grow a company, it maintains a slightly smaller one. The channel pays out in proportion to what you already have. It is the one marketing channel that is strictly worse for the people who need it most.

There is a second cap on top of the first, and in this trade it hurts more. Referrals arrive on the referrer's schedule. A deck referral happens when somebody is standing on a new deck admiring it, which is July, August and September. That is exactly when your crews are already booked out and you cannot take the work. In January, when you would trade almost anything for three appointments, nobody is standing in a backyard. That is the feast-or-famine cycle, and it is not a discipline problem. It is built into the channel.

Chip P. is the clean example again. He books work in November for six to eight months out while most of his competition waits for spring. That is not something a referral pipeline can do for you, because the referral has to wait for a finished deck and a visiting neighbor.

The Reward Question: What to Offer and What It Should Cost

Every article on this subject tells you to offer a gift card. Fine. Two things about it that nobody says.

First, the reward is almost never why someone refers you. People refer because the deck is good and they like you, and because recommending somebody competent makes them look good to their neighbor. A reward is a thank-you and a reminder, not an incentive. Price it accordingly. Something that is obviously generous relative to a thank-you, and obviously small relative to a deck, lands right. Something that reads like a commission makes an honest homeowner uncomfortable, and it makes the referral sound bought when they pass your name along.

Second, pay it on the signed job, not the inquiry, and say so in one plain sentence up front. The moment a referral program has terms that need explaining, you have built an administrative job for yourself and a reason for somebody to be annoyed with you later.

The version I would actually run: a real thank-you that shows up fast, something like a dinner out on you when the referred job signs, plus a free wash or a maintenance visit on their own deck. The second one is better than it looks, because it puts you back in their yard, where their neighbors can see you working, in the summer, when referrals happen.

The Neighbors Around a Deck You're Already Building

The highest-intent audience in the entire trade is the twenty houses around a deck you are building right now, and the window is open for about two weeks.

Those people are watching a project happen. They have seen your truck, your crew's behavior, and the finished result. They are the only strangers in your market who have real evidence about you. Ranked on cost per booked appointment, past customers and referrals come first and the neighbors around a job you are already building come immediately after.

Work it on purpose rather than hoping. Knock while the crew is there and the deck is half-built, which is when it is most interesting to look at. Ask the customer first, because some of them would rather you didn't. Leave something behind for the ones who are out. And if you run any paid social at all, the cheapest audience you will ever build is a one-mile radius around your active job sites with a photo of that job in the ad. Same evidence, more houses, and it does not depend on anyone being home when you knock.

Referral Partners Most Deck Companies Never Ask

Past customers are not the only people who can hand you a deck. Almost nobody in this trade works the other side, and it is the one part of referral generation that does not wait on your own job count.

  • Adjacent trades on the same house. Fence companies, hardscapers, pool builders, sunroom and patio cover installers. Same homeowner, same budget conversation, no competition. A pool builder is standing in a backyard that needs a deck roughly every week.
  • Real estate agents, but only the right ones. Listing agents want a deck fixed fast and cheap before photos, which is a repair business. Buyer's agents who sell older housing stock are the valuable ones, because their client just bought a house with a 20-year-old deck and a renovation budget.
  • Lumber yards and decking suppliers. The counter staff get asked "do you know anyone who installs this" constantly, and they answer with whoever they know. Being the name they know costs you showing up.
  • Property managers and HOAs. Lower margin, repetitive, and steady in the months when nothing else is.
  • The builder you lost to on price. Underrated. Every deck company has a minimum and a maximum. Send them the jobs below yours and they will send you the ones above theirs.

The one honest caveat: partner referrals take months to produce anything and they go cold if you stop showing up. It is a real channel, it is just not a channel you can turn on in a slow February.

How to Track Deck Referrals Without a CRM Project

You cannot know which ask worked if you do not know where anything came from, and this is where most builders quietly guess.

It does not need software. It needs one question asked the same way every time, on the first call: "how did you hear about us," and the answer written down before the call ends. Then one column in a spreadsheet, and once a quarter, five minutes counting.

Count three things per source, not one. How many inquiries it produced, how many of those sat down with you, and how many signed. Inquiry counts on their own will lie to you in both directions. For reference from a real deck account, Chris W., a deck builder in Gulf Shores, Alabama, spent $5,109.15 over about 60 days and got 106 homeowner inquiries, 42 completed sales appointments and 12 jobs won. Roughly 6 in 10 of those inquiries never sat down with anybody. If you only counted the top number you would have a completely wrong picture of that account, and the same gap exists in your referral column. You just cannot see it yet.

One more reason to track honestly: builders systematically over-credit referrals. The homeowner who says "a friend recommended you" often found you three other ways first and the friend confirmed it. That is still a referral and it is also a reason your other channels look weaker than they are.

What to Run While You Wait

Here is the honest version of the answer. If your business runs entirely on referrals, you do not have a marketing problem, you have a predictability problem. The fix is not replacing referrals. It is putting something underneath them that you can turn up in the months they cannot cover.

In order, cheapest first:

  1. Call your past customer list out loud. Not an email. This week, free, and it is the closest thing to a referral you can generate on demand.
  2. Fill out your Google Business Profile properly with real project photos, and ask every finished customer for a review on the day they are happiest. Reviews are referrals that work while you sleep.
  3. Work the active job site radius, as above, every single job.
  4. Then, and only then, put ads in front of homeowners who are not looking yet. This is the part referrals structurally cannot do, because a referral requires somebody to already be thinking about a deck.

That last one is where Chip P.'s story turned. Search volume was never his constraint. Being in front of homeowners who were not looking yet was. Within 21 days of launching a Meta ad system built for his trade, he converted more than $200,000 in booked work, four projects between $40,000 and $65,000. Buying shared leads before that, he was reaching about 2 of every 10 homeowners. On an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10. Same man, same phone, same trade.

And the qualification layer is what keeps that volume from eating your Saturdays. In his first three weeks on a qualification funnel, Chip got 63 homeowner inquiries and exactly one of the 63 questioned him about his minimum. State the minimum out loud and the people who cannot afford you stop booking.

Brian W. is the other side of the same point. His best lead source had been a home show, bringing in roughly 50 leads a year. On a built appointment system he sent 60 estimates in his first six weeks and closed about 25, roughly two estimates per job. His one regret was signing in mid-March instead of January. The Short Version

Ask on reveal day with a specific question, hand them something physical, and text them two weeks later. Email your past customer list twice a year. Work the twenty houses around every job you build. Build two or three partner relationships that do not depend on your own job count. Write down where every inquiry came from and count completed appointments, not inquiries.

Then accept the ceiling instead of fighting it. Referrals are a percentage of the decks you already finished, they arrive in the three months you are already full, and there is no lever to pull in January. Work them hard, expect them to pay you back in proportion to the size you already are, and build the predictable part underneath.

Deck Referrals: Common Questions

How many referrals should a deck builder expect per job?

Nobody has published a credible benchmark for this trade, so do not trust a number you read in an article, including mine. Put your own figures into the shape instead: referred bids per year is roughly your finished jobs times your referral rate per job, and signed referral jobs is that times your close rate. The useful part is not the rate, it is the structure. A builder finishing 12 decks a year cannot ask their way to 24, because the input is last year's job count, not this year's effort.

What is the best way to ask for a deck referral?

Ask a specific question at a specific moment. On reveal day, standing on the finished deck, ask who else on that street has been complaining about theirs. A general prompt like "if you know anybody, send them my way" fails because people cannot search their memory on a general prompt. Then text, not email, 10 to 14 days later, after they have had people over for the first weekend. That is the highest-intent window of the whole job, and almost nobody uses it.

Should deck builders pay for referrals?

Pay a thank-you, not a commission. People refer you because the deck is good and recommending somebody competent makes them look good to their neighbor, so the reward is a reminder rather than the reason. Keep it obviously generous next to a thank-you and obviously small next to a deck, pay it when the referred job signs rather than when it inquires, and state the whole thing in one sentence. The moment the terms need explaining you have built paperwork for yourself. A free wash or maintenance visit on their own deck is worth more than it looks, because it puts you back in their yard in summer where the neighbors can see you.

Why do deck referrals dry up in the winter?

Because a deck referral needs somebody standing on a new deck admiring it, and that happens in July, August and September. Which is exactly when your crews are already booked and you cannot take the work. In January, when three appointments would change your quarter, nobody is in a backyard. That is the feast-or-famine cycle, and it is built into the channel rather than being a discipline problem. It is also why the off-season is the cheapest window on the calendar: most of your competition goes dark, and homeowners plan decks in winter and build them in spring.

Can a deck company grow on referrals alone?

It can hold a size. It cannot reliably grow, because referral volume is a percentage of the jobs you already completed, so the channel pays out in proportion to what you already have. Chip P., a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California, had sent out five or six deck bids in the first ten months of 2025 running mostly on referrals, and he closes around 90% of the jobs he gets in front of. His business was never a selling problem, it was a volume problem, and more asking was not going to solve it. Referrals are the exhaust, not the engine.

Do referral leads close better than ads?

On trust, yes, and it is not close. A referred homeowner arrives having already decided you are the person. What a referral does not bring is any information about the project, so you will drive out to a referred homeowner with a $12,000 budget and a $45,000 idea and lose a Saturday, because a past customer sent them. A qualified appointment arrives with project type, budget range, timing and decision-makers established before it lands on your calendar. Referral-quality trust does not mean referral-quality project, and the two channels fail in opposite directions.

How do I track where my deck referrals came from?

One question on every first call, asked the same way, written down before the call ends: how did you hear about us. Then one spreadsheet column and five minutes of counting once a quarter. Count three numbers per source rather than one: inquiries, completed sales appointments and signed jobs. Inquiry counts on their own mislead badly. One deck account spent $5,109.15 over about 60 days and produced 106 inquiries, 42 completed appointments and 12 jobs won, so roughly 6 in 10 inquiries never sat down with anybody. Expect that same gap hiding in your referral column, and expect to be over-crediting referrals, because the homeowner who says a friend recommended you often found you three other ways first.
