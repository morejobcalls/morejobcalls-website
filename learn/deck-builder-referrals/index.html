How to Get More Deck Referrals (and Why Last Year Sets the Ceiling)

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

To get more deck referrals, stop asking harder and start asking at the right moment: at the final walkthrough, on the street you're already building on, once a year to every past customer, and every time a review comes in. Those raise the share of customers who send you someone. What they can't change is the base, because referral volume is capped by last year's job count.

So run the program below, and run one channel alongside it that doesn't care how many decks you built last year. That's the whole strategy.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. Referrals are the one channel I don't sell, and they are still the best work a deck company will ever get, so this is the referral program I'd run if I owned a deck company. It's also the honest arithmetic on how to get more deck referrals, including the part where the math stops cooperating.

Most advice on this topic is three tips and a flyer. The problem is that a $30,000 project is a different animal from a $400 repair, and the referral plays that work for a handyman feel cheap on a deck. Every number here came out of a named client's mouth.

Why Referral Volume Is Capped by Last Year's Job Count

Every referral starts with a customer. That sounds obvious until you follow it through: the only people who can refer you this year are the households you built for already. If you finished 20 decks last year, 20 households can vouch for you. A great program raises how many of them do it. It can't turn 20 into 60.

That's why referrals feel like feast or famine even in a good company. A slow year produces a slow referral pipeline twelve months later, at the exact moment you can least afford it, and no amount of asking harder breaks the loop. I wrote the broad version of this in how to get more deck jobs. This page is only the referral piece, worked all the way through.

One of my clients, Mike, is the cleanest example. Great work, steady referrals, and he couldn't tell you whether next month would do $50K or $150K. He sat at the same revenue level for five years because of it. He wasn't bad at referrals. He was excellent at them. That was the whole problem.

Chris Walters and his dad ran E&C Custom Homes in Gulf Shores, Alabama as a word-of-mouth deck company that had never hired an agency, and he described the ceiling better than I can: "Word of mouth is great, it keeps you busy, but it's never going to take you to the level that you want it to be."

Brian Wallace at Bend Fence & Deck had the same cap with a home show instead of a referral base: 40 or 50 leads once a year, then quiet. Six weeks into a channel that didn't depend on the show, he'd done more appointments than all of last year. The base moved. His craftsmanship didn't.

Three things fall out of that table. Doubling your referral rate roughly doubles referral jobs, for almost no money. The close rate is already high, because a referred homeowner arrives with the trust question answered (the rule of thumb I use across this site: referrals close at 70 to 90%, cold leads at 10 to 30%). And the only lever that changes the order of magnitude is the first column, which is last year, which you can't touch.

That's the whole reason this page has a section at the bottom about what to run alongside referrals. The program raises the rate. Something else has to raise the base.

How to Get More Deck Referrals: A Program Built for a $25K to $80K Ticket

Six parts. The first four cost hours, not money. Do them in order.

1. Ask at the right moment, with a specific question

The moment is the final walkthrough, in person, before the last payment clears. The homeowner is standing on the thing they've pictured for months, the goodwill is at its peak, and you're right there. Not an email a month later. Not a line at the bottom of the invoice.

The question matters as much as the timing. "Let me know if you know anyone" asks the homeowner to do the sorting and produces nothing. Ask something they can answer on the spot: "Who do you know who's been talking about their backyard this year?" People usually have a name. Get it, and ask whether they'd rather make the introduction or let you mention them.

Then ask twice more: on the first warm weekend after completion, when the deck gets its first party, and the following spring, when the whole street starts thinking about their own yard. Three asks, all timed to moments when the deck is already the topic.

2. The neighbor play around an active job

This is the most underworked play in decking. While your crew is on site, the eight or ten houses that can see the work are watching a deck go up in real time: the framing, the material, how clean the site is, whether your guys show up at 7 or at 10. It's the most convincing ad you'll ever run and it's free.

Put a sign in the yard the day the lumber lands, with permission. Knock the doors that can see the job early in the build and introduce yourself as the guy building next door. Don't sell: you'll be here two weeks, here's a card, come look when it's done. Do a second pass on the last day with a photo of the finished deck, and ask the customer if a couple of neighbors can walk it. Most people are proud of a new deck and say yes, and a neighbor standing on it while the owner says the price was fair is a referral and a site visit at once.

3. Past-customer reactivation

Your past customer list is the most underworked asset in a deck company. Decks get refinished, extended, railed, screened, roofed over, and the homeowner who trusted you with $30,000 four years ago is the easiest yes in your pipeline. Most builders have never once called them.

Call every past customer once a year, in the off-season, when you have time and they're planning. Three parts: how's the deck holding up, anything they've thought about adding, and who on their street or in their family has mentioned wanting one. That last question lands better on a call that started with you checking on their deck than on one that started with you wanting something. If you're a word-of-mouth company, this list is your whole marketing department. Treat it like one.

4. The review-to-referral loop

A review is a referral to strangers. It's also a signal that a specific customer is happy right now, which is the exact moment to ask for a referral to someone they know.

So close the loop. Every review gets a reply within a day and a text from the owner, not the office: thank you, that meant a lot, and if anyone you know is thinking about their backyard I'd be glad to talk to them. The ask is one introduction, not "spread the word," which is a favor with no shape.

Reviews matter for a second reason: a referred homeowner still looks you up before they call, and if Google shows 11 reviews from 2021, some of that referral leaks out before it reaches your phone. Ask for the review at the final walkthrough, the same visit as the referral ask, every job.

5. Realtor, pool builder and landscaper partnerships

The trades that touch the same backyard are the partners worth having, and most deck builders leave them alone because it feels awkward. It isn't. A pool builder finishing an install has a customer who now needs a deck. A landscaper doing hardscape has a customer already spending on the yard. A realtor closing on a house with a rotten deck knows before anyone.

Pick three to five, not thirty. Walk them through a finished job and keep the deal simple: you send me the deck conversations, I send you everything I hear about pools, planting and people moving. Send work back first, because that's the only thing that keeps a partnership alive past the first month. And show up fast when they refer you; a slow response embarrasses the partner in front of their customer, and you don't get a second one.

6. A referral thank-you that isn't tacky

Here's where the handyman playbook falls apart. A printed cash bounty on a $30,000 project reads as small, and worse, it turns a friend's recommendation into a sales pitch. Nobody wants to tell their neighbor "use my deck guy, I get a kickback."

What works is a thank-you that's personal, a little generous, and arrives after the referred job signs: a handwritten note from the owner, something for the deck itself, a first-season clean and inspection. The value of the gift isn't the point. It should feel like gratitude from a business the homeowner is proud to have used, because that pride is what made them refer you, and a coupon cheapens it.

The Two Things a Referral Still Needs From You

A referral is the easiest appointment you'll ever run, and you can still lose it two ways. Speed: a referred homeowner who texts you Thursday at 4pm was told you're great, and if they don't hear back until Monday, the referrer's word is competing with the two other builders they found while waiting. Ricardo Cervantes, a concrete and deck contractor in Colorado, runs automatic reminders under every appointment, and in his words, "our follow-up is top-of-the-line compared to other contractors." The point isn't the software. It's that nobody waits. The whole response-time problem is in why homeowners stop picking up the phone.

The price on site: a referral arrives closer to a yes than any other homeowner, and emailing a bid three days later throws that away. The builders I work with who present the price in the backyard, before they leave, took close rates from around 15% to over 40%. On a referral, the trust was already there. Don't waste it.

What to Run While You Wait for Referrals to Compound

Back to the first column. The program raises the rate. The only thing that raises the base is doing more decks this year than last, which takes a channel that turns on this week and doesn't care what last year looked like.

For an established deck builder, that's your own ad account, with the owner on camera, pointed at a calendar the homeowner books themselves. I run these, so weigh that accordingly. The reason it belongs on a referral page is that it produces what a referral produces, just earlier: a homeowner who feels like they know you before the first call.

Josiah Walker runs Western Rockies Construction in a Montana market of about 90,000 people across eight towns, and he'd been advertising in a free local newspaper with one job left on the books. Eight months in, at 40 to 50 opportunities a month and a crew of six plus a sub crew, what he brought up unprompted wasn't a number: "When we're walking around Costco and people are recognising my hat — 'Oh, Western Rockies, is that your guys' company? I know we're going to call you guys.'" That's a referral base built from the top down, in a town where the bottom-up version was one job. When the person in the ad is the person on the estimate, the homeowner arrives feeling like a referral instead of a cold inquiry. His first six weeks produced 60 estimates and about 25 closed jobs, and every one of those 25 households is a referral source next year that didn't exist this year. That's what raising the base looks like.

Jacob Weaver at Oasis Custom Decks in Pennsylvania crossed $1 million for the first time, closing $480,000 in 21 days along the way, with crews booked five months out. Chris Walters, the word-of-mouth builder from the top of this page, booked $320,000 in deck jobs in about 60 days on $100 a day and paused the ads to hire. Both will have a bigger referral year next year than any program alone could have given them, because the first column moved.

The channel that raises the base arrives as a wave, not a line: Chris ran 13 to 14 appointments a week and had 40 or 50 inquiries he never got to call back, which is 40 or 50 future referral sources lost to a full calendar. Know who runs the estimates before you turn anything on. And keep the ad account, the CRM and the customer list in your name, because that list is next year's referral program. The Short Version

Ask at the final walkthrough with a question the homeowner can answer on the spot. Work the street you're already building on, with the owner doing the knocking. Call every past customer once a year. Turn every review into a request for one named introduction. Build three to five partners who touch the same backyard and send them work first. Thank people like it's a $30,000 project, because it is.

Then accept what the math says. The program raises your rate and it's worth every hour, but it can't raise last year's job count, and that's the base. Run something alongside it that doesn't depend on last year, and next year's referral program will be the biggest you've ever had.
