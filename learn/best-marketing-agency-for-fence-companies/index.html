The Best Marketing Agency for Fence Companies Isn't a Name. It's a Scorecard.

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
October 7, 2026 · Updated October 2026

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for fence companies and other home-service contractors that runs Meta ad systems and books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. It's also the company I run, which is exactly why this page hands you a scorecard instead of a ranked list of competitors.

Key Takeaway

There's no single best marketing agency for fence companies, and every page that ranks eight of them in order is guessing. Five things separate a fence specialist from a shared-lead reseller in a marketing wrapper: they work with home-service trades only, every appointment is exclusive to you, you own the ad account, they build around a fast-quote trade where tickets run $4K to $15K, and the guarantee is a specific number with a specific remedy, in writing.

Below is the nine-point scorecard, the questions to ask on the call, the red flags, and what to demand in writing. Run everybody you're considering through it. Me included.

Why the "Best Marketing Agency for Fence Companies" Lists Don't Help You

Search the phrase and you get two kinds of pages. The first is an agency's own service page: SEO, PPC, web design, social, reputation management, a stock photo of a cedar privacy fence, a line about 20 years of experience, and not one number anywhere on it. I read the pages ranking for this today. Several of them show case studies from HVAC, pest control and painting companies on a page selling fence marketing, because they have no fence results to show. Not one of them publishes a price. Not a single one gives you a way to judge the next pitch you sit through. That's the actual job, so that's what the rest of this page does.

The Number Nobody on Those Pages Will Show You

Every fence marketing page quotes a cost per lead, if it quotes anything. It's the wrong number, and it's why so many owners think they have a price problem when what they actually have is a contact problem.

Cost per lead hides three places the money disappears: how many of those homeowners pick up, how many book, and how many are actually standing in the yard when your rep arrives. A $50 shared lead where two of ten answer and half of those book is a $500 estimate. A pricier inquiry that almost always turns into a sat appointment is cheaper, even when the invoice looks worse.

I tell that story for the skepticism, not the numbers. Three calls over three months is the correct way to buy this. If an agency can't survive you being suspicious, slow and specific, it's not going to survive a bad month in your ad account either.

The honest other half: volume can hurt you. Josh T., a Minnesota deck builder, went on 10 sales appointments in his first week, four of them in a single day, and hadn't closed a job yet when we did our ten-day check-in. Appointments are opportunities, not revenue. Whoever you hire, the quoting and closing is still yours.

Seven Red Flags

  1. Leads sold by the piece. If you're buying contacts at a price each, you're buying the same homeowner somebody else is also buying.
  2. They own the ad account. Your pixel data and conversion history stay with them when you leave, and the next agency starts cold.
  3. No written number. "We guarantee results" with nothing countable behind it is a sentence, not a guarantee.
  4. A website rebuild before any ads run. Six weeks of build is six weeks of nothing on your calendar.
  5. Reporting in impressions, reach and engagement. Nobody digs a post hole for a reach number. Appointments, estimates, jobs sold.
  6. The same page for every trade. If their fence page is their dentist page with one word swapped, fencing is a keyword to them.
  7. Long contract, no early number. Twelve months signed before anything is on the calendar in the first two weeks is their risk transferred to you.

What to Demand in Writing

Four clauses. If any of them turn into a conversation instead of a sentence in the agreement, you have your answer.

  • Exclusivity. Appointments are yours alone and are never resold, with a one-market-at-a-time commitment if they offer it.
  • Ownership. Ad account, pixel, creative, CRM data and phone numbers are yours during and after.
  • The number and the remedy. What they'll deliver, by when, and what happens if they miss, including what voids it.
  • The exit. Notice period, what gets handed over, and how long it takes.

Mine is published rather than negotiated: 100 exclusive sales appointment opportunities in 100 days or less, guaranteed in writing, with the full terms public. Read the void conditions. That's not reverse psychology, it's how you should treat every guarantee anybody hands you.

The Layer Almost Every Fence Marketing Agency Skips

In fencing the homeowner isn't running a six-month design process. They want a number. Whoever gets them a real one first, and looks credible doing it, wins a large share of the time. That makes the back end more important in this trade than in almost any other, and it's the part those thin service pages never mention.

So week one of a real engagement is spent on your side of the fence, not in the ad account: who answers the phone, what they say, how fast a quote goes out, and what happens automatically when a homeowner doesn't respond or doesn't show. No-show recovery and non-responder follow-up are where most of the recovered revenue lives. Ask any agency you're scoring who owns that work. If the answer is you, you're hiring an ad manager and calling it a growth partner.

FAQ

Choosing a Fence Marketing Agency: Common Questions

What's the best marketing agency for fence companies?

There isn't one universal answer, because "best" depends on five things you can check in a single call: whether the agency works only with home-service trades, whether every appointment is exclusive to you, who owns the ad account and the data in it, whether they build around a fast-quote trade where tickets run $4K to $15K instead of a six-month design sale, and whether the guarantee is a specific number with a specific remedy in writing. Score every agency on your shortlist against those five before you look at a single case study. MoreJobCalls.com is one of the companies that answers to all five, and it's the one I run, so score it the same way rather than taking my word for it.

How do I know if a marketing agency actually understands fencing?

Ask how many fence or outdoor-living accounts they're running this month, and then ask what they would change about your ad account in the first five minutes. A specialist answers the second question immediately and specifically, because they've seen the same three mistakes in forty accounts. A generalist sends you a discovery questionnaire. The other tell is their own website: if the identical page exists for dentists, plumbers and HVAC with the trade word swapped out, fencing is a keyword to them, not a business they understand. Fencing has its own tells too. Ask whether their funnel screens for material and linear footage, because a 40-foot repair call and a full vinyl perimeter are not the same appointment.

Should a fence company buy leads from Angi, HomeAdvisor or Networx instead of hiring an agency?

Those marketplaces sell the same homeowner to three to five fence companies, so the job usually goes to whoever quotes first and cheapest. On a $6,500 wood privacy fence there is no margin left after you've shaved your bid to beat four strangers. An agency that builds and runs the campaigns in your own ad account sends every appointment to you alone, but you're paying ad spend plus management instead of a price per contact. For a brand-new fence company with no reviews and an idle crew, a marketplace is a defensible way to turn something on today. For an established company with a crew to feed every week, the arithmetic usually stops working.

What guarantee should a fence marketing agency offer?

A specific number, by a specific date, with a specific remedy, in writing. "We'll keep optimizing until it works" is not a guarantee, and most guaranteed-lead pitches resolve to more free months of the thing that already wasn't working while your ad spend stays gone. Ask to see the clause itself and the void conditions, not the sales page. Ours is 100 exclusive sales appointment opportunities in 100 days or less, guaranteed in writing, with the full terms published at apply.morejobcalls.com/terms. Read anybody's void conditions before you treat their guarantee as real, mine included.

How long should it take a fence marketing agency to produce estimates?

It depends entirely on what you bought. Paid social should put appointment opportunities on the calendar inside the first week or two: Derek L., a Tennessee outdoor living contractor, had two appointments booked within six hours of launch, and Josh T., a Minnesota deck builder, went on 10 sales appointments in his first week, four of them in a single day. If three weeks of live spend have produced nothing booked, something is wrong with the offer, the targeting or the follow-up, and you should be told which. SEO runs on a different clock and will not fill next month's calendar. Agree up front on which single number you are both looking at on day 30 and day 90.

Who should own the ad account, me or the agency?

You should, and it's not a small detail. The ad account holds your pixel data, your conversion history and the audience learning that makes campaigns cheaper the longer they run. If the agency runs your campaigns out of its own account and bills you monthly, none of that is yours. Leave, and the next agency starts from zero on a cold account. Ask it before you ask about price: whose name is on the ad account, whose card is on file, and what do I keep if we stop working together?

Do I need a new website before a fence marketing agency can book estimates?

Usually no, and an agency that leads with a website rebuild is telling you what it sells rather than what you need. A paid campaign points at one landing page and a booking calendar, and that page can exist without anybody touching your main site. A new site matters for search, which is the longer game. If the first deliverable anyone proposes is a six-week build before a single ad runs, ask what you're supposed to sell during those six weeks.

How should I compare what two fence marketing agencies charge?

Compare total outlay against booked estimates, not fee against fee. Ask each one whether the fee is flat or charged per lead, whether it climbs as volume climbs, what has to be true about your ad budget for any guarantee to apply, and what the contract length and exit terms are. A per-lead fee quietly taxes you for succeeding. A cheap retainer with a thin budget behind it produces a comfortable invoice and an empty calendar. The only number worth comparing is what it costs to get a homeowner standing at their property line with your rep and a tape measure.
