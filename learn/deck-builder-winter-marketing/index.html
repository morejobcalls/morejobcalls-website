Deck Builder Winter Marketing: The October-to-March Playbook for a Full Spring Calendar

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Winter is the cheapest, least-contested window on a deck builder's calendar, and the plan for it is simple: keep the ads on from October through March, sell the spring build instead of the winter install, take deposits for spring slots, and work the customers you already have. Homeowners decide on a $40K to $100K deck in winter and build it in spring, so whoever is in the conversation in January holds the contract in April.

The proof is on this site already. One client put about $1.2M on the books by February 25 on roughly $10K in ad spend while the biggest competitor in his market couldn't keep seven crews busy. Chip Paynter had 63 opportunities come through in his first three weeks, in November. Justin Wylie: "I've never experienced a January and a February that went this well. Never." Below is the month-by-month version of what they did.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. Most deck companies call me for the first time in October, when the phone goes quiet, asking some version of "do I shut it all down until March?" This page is the answer: a deck builder winter marketing plan, month by month, October through March. It's a playbook, not a case study. The case study is how one client put $1.2M on the books by February 25, and I'll quote its numbers where they matter instead of retelling it.

Why Winter Is the Cheapest Window a Deck Builder Gets

Every reason below is the same reason from a different angle: your competitors leave, and the homeowners don't.

  • Attention is on sale. Most deck companies pause their ads from November to March. Fewer builders bidding for the same homeowners means your daily budget buys more of the feed, exactly when the planners are sitting inside looking at the backyard they didn't use enough last summer.
  • Nobody builds a deck in January. Plenty of people decide on one. A $40K to $100K project gets planned for weeks or months, and much of that planning happens in winter. The decision window opens in January; the shovel hits dirt in April. Show up when the shovel moves and you missed the decision by three months.
  • Competitors are dark, and so are their calendars. In the $1.2M case, the biggest competitor in the market, a builder claiming $6 to $7M a year with seven crews, told my client he was struggling to keep his guys busy. Everyone else was "dead in the water." Their crews were calling my client for work.
  • Search can't create demand. Almost nobody types "deck builder near me" in January. Social ads reach the homeowner still daydreaming about summer; the client in the case study named that switch, from search to social, as his biggest change.
  • A January appointment is a spring build. Sell in winter and you walk into March with a backlog, quoting from strength instead of shaving margin to keep the lights on.

None of this needs a bigger budget than summer. It needs the budget left on. The channel-by-channel reasoning, including where search and SEO still fit, is in the deck builder marketing guide; the rest of this page assumes you're staying visible and want to know what to do each month.

Ads: same budget, different message

The winter ad is the owner, on camera, in front of a finished deck, saying what the spring slot costs and what it takes to reserve it. Not "build a deck now." The investment range goes in on purpose: it costs you the kit-price shoppers and tells the platform who to find more of. Behind the ad sits an application that asks project type, budget range, timeline and property ownership before it shows a calendar, which is why exactly one of Chip Paynter's 63 November opportunities questioned him about his minimum. The structure and creative are in how Justin Wylie went from $400 to under $30 per lead in 14 days; nothing about it changes in winter except the words.

Past-customer reactivation: the easiest yes you have

Your past-customer list is the most underworked asset in a deck company, and winter is when you have time to work it. Decks get refinished, extended, railed, screened and roofed over. The homeowner who trusted you with $30,000 four years ago is the easiest yes in your pipeline, and most builders have never called them. Two touches: a thank-you in November with one question, "what's next for the backyard?", and a January note offering a spring slot before you open them to new customers. Ask for the referral out loud on both.

Reviews: bank them before the season ends

The winter buyer has time, and uses it to read. Every job that finishes in October and November should produce a Google review and photos while the homeowner is still standing on the new deck. This isn't for SEO; it's so the homeowner who sees your January ad and searches your name finds recent, specific reviews instead of a handful from years ago.

Financing: the bridge from "next year" to "this spring"

A homeowner who decides on a deck in January and hasn't budgeted for it can wait a year or finance it. If you were the only builder who mentioned a monthly payment, you get the spring slot. Have the lender relationship in place by December, put "financing available" in the winter creative, and present the monthly number on every winter quote, not just when asked. The point is that the conversation happens on your appointment in January instead of a competitor's in June.

Spring slots with deposits: the play that makes the rest pay

This is what turns a winter appointment into a February bank balance. The offer is specific: design done now, permits moving, first on the schedule when the ground thaws, reserved with a signed contract and a deposit. Cap the slots you announce so the scarcity is real. The deposit commits the homeowner while they're motivated, puts cash in the account in the month you need it most, and gives you a production schedule to hire and order materials against. Billy Stewart at Trinity Decks wrote $300,000 or more in contracts and deposits in 17 days on less than $1,657 of ad spend; the deposits are what made the backlog real rather than hopeful.

Content and video: feed the machine

Film every active site in October while you still have them, then film the design meetings and the shop in winter. The footage becomes the ads, and the appointments go better because the homeowner feels they already know you. Derek Lopez at Highline Outdoor Living put his face in the ad and the age objection he'd heard at every estimate mostly stopped coming up; the same effect answers "are you even open in January?"

Capacity cuts both ways, so plan for it. Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes ran 13 to 14 sales appointments a week on about $100 a day, had 40 to 50 inquiries he never got to call back, got booked out 10 weeks and paused the ads to hire; the checklist is in the $320K in 60 days case. That was summer, with him on job sites; the same volume in January lands on a calendar you can actually service. The client in the $1.2M case was running about 90 appointments a month, and when we talked about adding 210 more, the question wasn't whether the appointments existed; it was whether his operation could absorb them. Decide your ceiling before you turn the ads up, and hire in February and March, not April.

On cash, do the margin math conservatively. In the case study I made the client cut it to a real net number after running costs, 12 to 15 percent rather than the 25 to 30 percent gross figure, and it still came to roughly $150K in his pocket in two months on about $10K in ads. Your version of that arithmetic is the budget case for staying on through winter. What Not to Do in the Deck Builder Slow Season

  1. Don't turn the ads off in November. The single biggest mistake. You hand every spring signature to whoever stayed visible, and you reset the ad account's learning so March starts from zero at peak prices.
  2. Don't sell the winter install. "Build a deck now" in January reads as desperate and gets ignored. "Reserve your spring slot" reads as in demand and gets booked.
  3. Don't move the winter budget to search. Search harvests the handful of people typing "deck builder near me" in January; it can't create the buyer who hasn't started looking.
  4. Don't drop the qualifying questions to get more volume. A frictionless form fills a winter calendar with browsers. Keep budget, timeline and ownership on the application; let the cheaper auction supply the volume.
  5. Don't discount to fill winter. A signed backlog is what lets you hold price. Cutting margin in January to "stay busy" is how you end up with a full spring of skinny jobs.
  6. Don't buy shared leads to bridge the gap. A $50 shared lead at a 2-in-10 contact rate is about $500 per sat appointment, before you count the no-shows, and the same homeowner went to three other builders. If you're on a platform now, here are the alternatives compared.
  7. Don't lay off the crew and expect them back. Go quiet for four months and your best guys find work elsewhere. Winter marketing is a hiring strategy wearing a marketing costume.

FAQ

Deck Builder Winter Marketing: Common Questions

When should a deck builder start winter marketing?

October, before the phone goes quiet, not January after it has. The homeowner who wants a deck by Memorial Day starts deciding in winter, and the builder who is already visible when that decision window opens is the one holding the contract in April. Starting in October also means your ads have been running and learning for weeks by the time competitors go dark in November, which is when the cheapest attention of the year shows up.

Is it worth running ads for a deck company in January?

It is the highest-return month of the year for the builders who do it, because almost nobody else is bidding for the same homeowners. One deck builder client ran straight through the winter and by February 25 had roughly $300K cash collected, $200K of work on the street and over $700K signed, about $1.2M on the books before March, off roughly $10K in ad spend. Justin Wylie of All Pro Decks and Patios put it this way after his first winter on the system: "I've never experienced a January and a February that went this well. Never."

What should a deck builder's winter ads say?

Sell the spring build, not the winter install. The January message is "lock your spring slot now: design done, permits moving, first on the schedule when the ground thaws," said by the owner on camera in front of a finished project. Include a realistic investment range so kit-price shoppers filter themselves out, and send the homeowner to an application that asks project type, budget range, timeline and property ownership before it shows them a calendar. Search ads are the wrong tool here because almost nobody types "deck builder near me" in January; social ads reach the homeowner who is still deciding.

How do you keep cash flow up during the deck builder slow season?

Three levers, in order. Sell spring slots in December and January and take a deposit with the signed contract, so cash arrives months before the build. Work your past-customer list for refinishes, extensions, screens and covers that can be quoted and scheduled in winter. And keep the ads on so the backlog stacks up, because a signed backlog is what lets you quote from strength instead of cutting margin to keep the lights on. The client in the $1.2M case had roughly $300K in cash collected for the year by February 25.

Should a deck builder take deposits for spring slots in winter?

Yes. A signed contract with a deposit in January is what turns a winter appointment into a spring build and a February bank balance. It commits the homeowner while they are motivated, it gives you a real production schedule to hire and buy materials against, and it is the single biggest difference between a builder who walks into March with a backlog and one who walks in with a blank calendar. Billy Stewart at Trinity Decks wrote $300,000 or more in contracts and deposits in 17 days on less than $1,657 of ad spend; the deposit is what made those numbers real.

What is the biggest winter marketing mistake deck builders make?

Turning the ads off in November and turning them back on in March, which is exactly when every other deck builder in the market is bidding up the same inventory. Going dark hands every spring signature to whoever stayed visible, it resets the ad account's learning so you start from zero in the most expensive month, and it sends your best crew members looking for work that some of them never come back from. The second biggest mistake is running winter ads that say "build a deck now" instead of "reserve your spring slot."

How much should a deck builder spend on ads in the off-season?

Roughly the same as in season, which for a paid social campaign in a high-ticket trade is around $50 to $100 a day, with about $3,000 a month as the floor at which the campaign gets enough data to work. The point of winter is not to spend less; it is that the same spend buys more attention when competitors are paused. Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes ran about $100 a day and booked roughly $320,000 in deck jobs in about 60 days on $5,109.15 of total spend, and the same daily budget stretches further in January than it does in April.
