Who Guarantees Appointments for Contractors?

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Four kinds of companies guarantee appointments for contractors: pay-per-appointment sellers and marketplaces (you pay, or get a credit, only when an appointment happens), performance shops that charge only after a job or deposit, monthly-fee agencies that give their management fee back if they miss a written number, and a smaller group that owes you a fixed cash payment if they miss.

MoreJobCalls.com is in that last group. The four are not equivalent. What separates them is one question: if you miss, what do you owe me, and where is that written? A credit, a fee back, and a check are three very different answers.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I have an obvious stake in this question, and one of the guarantees below is ours. I'll flag it when we get there. Everything I say about other companies comes from their own public pages as of September 2026, linked so you can check it yourself.

Every contractor I talk to has heard some version of it: guaranteed appointments, pay only for results. Most have signed at least one and gotten nothing they could take to the bank. The word "guarantee" is doing two jobs. Sometimes it's a promise with teeth, and sometimes it's a mood.

Who Actually Offers a Contractor Appointment Guarantee

Strip away the wording and every contractor appointment guarantee falls into one of these types. The column that matters most is the third one, because the exclusions are where most guarantees quietly die.

A guarantee is a risk transfer. If no risk moved from you to them, nothing was guaranteed.

The Ad Spend Question (Where Most People Get Confused)

"Money back" almost never means what contractors think it means.

On any clean setup, you pay the ad platform directly from your own card. That money goes from you to Meta or Google and never passes through the agency. So when an agency gives money back, it's giving back its fee. It physically can't hand back spend it never held.

That's not a trick, it's just how the money moves. Two things to check:

  1. Does the contract separate fees from spend in writing? If it just says "money back" or "100% back" with no definition, that ambiguity is not in your favor.
  2. Is the agency holding your ad budget at all? If they run ads on their own card and bill you, you don't own the ad account, the audience data, or the history. Walking away costs you everything you built. That's a bigger problem than the guarantee.

What Counts as an "Appointment"?

This is the definition that decides whether a guarantee is worth anything. I've seen all of these used:

  • A homeowner who agreed to a day and time.
  • An appointment that actually sat, meaning you met the homeowner.
  • An appointment where you issued, demoed, or bid.
  • A signed job or collected deposit.

Stricter definitions protect you more on price, but they also mean fewer things count toward the number. The part people skip: whether the appointment is exclusive. A homeowner who booked with you and three other companies is a bidding war, not an appointment. On a $30,000 deck that difference is everything.

Read the Void Conditions Before the Remedy

Every performance guarantee is conditional, and reasonably so. Nobody can promise appointments if you won't run ads or won't call anybody back. But the conditions are also the escape hatch. Common ones, most of them fair:

  • A minimum daily ad budget, kept running without gaps
  • A response-time requirement, usually calling new appointments within 24 hours
  • Showing up to onboarding and strategy calls
  • Not making unapproved changes to campaigns or landing pages
  • Giving accurate pricing, service-area and targeting info
  • Keeping payment current

Ask honestly whether your operation can hold up its end. If nobody answers the phone within a day, you'll have voided it in week two anyway.

The tell for a bad-faith version is conditions so vague that the company decides after the fact whether you met them. "Client must reasonably cooperate" is not a condition. It's an opinion.

What to Demand in Writing

Whoever you're talking to, me included, get these five things in the contract. Not the sales page. The contract.

  1. The definition of an appointment. Booked, sat, or bid? Exclusive to you, or shared?
  2. The time window. A number of appointments in a number of days, with a start date that's clear (launch day, not signing day).
  3. The void conditions, listed. Specific and measurable, not "reasonable cooperation."
  4. Whether ad spend is included. Fees only, spend only, both, or neither, spelled out.
  5. How the remedy is paid. Credit, fee back, or a check. How much, and how many days after the window closes.

One more worth asking out loud: whose name is on the ad account? If it's theirs, the guarantee is the smaller of your problems. Any company worth hiring answers all of this without flinching. The ones that get vague on the contract clause are telling you what you need to know.

That's the real job of a guarantee. It doesn't prove a company is good. It makes being wrong about them affordable.

FAQ

Contractor Appointment Guarantees: Common Questions

Who guarantees appointments for contractors?

Four kinds of companies do, and they guarantee different things. Pay-per-appointment sellers and marketplaces charge only when an appointment happens and credit ones that don't qualify. Performance shops charge only after a job or deposit. Monthly-fee agencies put a number in the contract and give their management fee back if they miss it. A smaller group, MoreJobCalls.com among them, owes you a fixed cash payment if they miss. The useful question is not who guarantees appointments, it's what they owe you when they miss and where that is written.

What is a contractor appointment guarantee?

It's a written promise that a marketing company will put a stated number of sales appointments on your calendar within a stated time, plus a remedy if it doesn't. A real one has four parts: a definition of what counts as an appointment, a time window, the conditions that void it, and a remedy you can measure, like a credit, your fee back, or a fixed cash amount. If any of those four is missing or vague, treat it as a sales line, not a guarantee.

Does an appointment guarantee cover my ad spend?

Almost never, and on a clean setup it can't. When you pay Meta or Google directly from your own card, that money never passes through the agency, so there is nothing for them to hand back. Fee-back guarantees return the management fee only. Pay-per-appointment models avoid the question because the ad cost is baked into the per-appointment price. If a contract just says money back without saying fees or spend, ask them to write the difference down before you sign.

What voids a contractor appointment guarantee?

The usual list is a minimum daily ad budget kept running without gaps, calling new appointments back within a set time (often 24 hours), showing up to onboarding and strategy calls, not changing campaigns or landing pages without telling them, giving accurate service-area and pricing info, and staying current on payment. Most of those are fair. The ones to push back on are vague, like "client must reasonably cooperate," because then the agency decides after the fact whether you qualified.

What counts as an appointment under a guarantee?

Whatever the contract says, so read that definition first. Some companies count a homeowner who agreed to a set day and time. Some only count appointments that sit or demo. Some don't charge until you sell or collect a deposit. Stricter definitions protect you on price but count fewer appointments toward the number. Either way, make sure what's being counted is exclusive to you and not a record sent to three other companies.

Is pay-per-appointment better than a monthly agency with a guarantee?

It depends on your ticket size and whether you want to own the machine. Pay-per-appointment is simple and low-risk to start, but you usually don't own the ad account or the data, and they set the price. A monthly agency with a written guarantee costs more up front, but on a good setup you keep the ad account, audiences, and history if you leave. For high-ticket trades like decks, where one job can be $20,000 to $50,000, owning the system usually wins over time.
