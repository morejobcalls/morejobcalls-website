Deck Builder Leads: What They Really Cost in 2026

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
September 11, 2026
Key Takeaway

The price on a deck builder lead is almost never what the lead costs you. A $50 shared lead where only 2 of 10 people pick up is a $250 conversation, and if half of those book, it's a $500 appointment before you've burned a drop of diesel. Contact rate and exclusivity move your real cost far more than the per-lead price does.

So the number to buy on isn't cost per lead. It's cost per appointment that actually sits down with you, with both decision-makers at the table. Ask every vendor for that number. Most can't produce it.

Full disclosure up front: I sell one of the things on this page. My company builds Meta ad systems for home service contractors, deck builders first. I've spent over $10 million on ads for contractors and I've audited hundreds of their ad accounts. I'm still going to tell you when buying leads is the right call, because sometimes it is, and because you've probably already been sold to enough.

Here's the problem with how deck builder leads get shopped. Everybody compares the sticker price. Forty-one dollars here, ninety there, two hundred over there. That comparison is useless, because a lead isn't a job and it isn't even a conversation. It's a phone number that may or may not belong to someone who remembers filling out the form.

What Deck Builder Leads Actually Cost, Source by Source

Three broad categories, and they behave completely differently.

Shared marketplaces (Angi, HomeAdvisor, Thumbtack, CraftJack) sell the same homeowner's contact info to several contractors at once, typically three to five. You pay per lead whether you reach the person or not. Decking leads on these platforms commonly run $40 to $150.

Deck lead vendors are the pay-per-lead companies that rank for decking terms. They run their own ads, then sell you the results. The ones on page one right now publish rates from about $41 to $200 per lead, and $75 to $150 per booked appointment, sometimes with a setup fee on top. Most describe their leads as exclusive. Some genuinely are.

Search-driven leads in a competitive metro go higher. Justin Wylie owns All Pro Decks & Patios in San Antonio. Before he found me he'd hired six other agencies, and the math they left him with was roughly $400 per lead at about 15 leads a month, all from Google, with the homeowner having already talked to three or four other contractors by the time he called.

The Number Nobody Puts on the Invoice: Contact Rate

Chip Paynter is a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California. Firefighter, paramedic, answers every call. When we first met he told me these platforms used to work great for him and then slowly got worse. He'd buy 10 leads and maybe two would pick up.

He also told me this, and I think about it a lot: "We talked to Yelp 18 different times. Whether we spent $50 a month or $1,500 a month, the leads were about the same either way."

Of course they were. Platforms get paid when someone fills out a form, not when you close a job. So they optimize for form fills and send you whatever comes with that. Your spend goes up, the quality doesn't, because quality was never the thing being purchased.

Now run the arithmetic on Chip's old contact rate. This is an illustration, not a client result, but the inputs are real.

That last row isn't a rounding error. One of Chip's old leads sent his son out to bid a deck on a lot that turned out to be bare dirt. No house. The homeowner wanted a bid on a house that hadn't been built yet. Diesel truck burning $100 to $150 in fuel, half an afternoon gone, for a project that doesn't exist. That cost is real and it never shows up in a cost-per-lead report.

Here's what changed for Chip. In the first ten months of 2025 he'd sent out maybe five or six deck bids, running mostly on referrals. We moved him off shared leads onto an exclusive qualification funnel, and his contact rate went from reaching 2 of 10 to talking with at least 8 of 10. He was usually the first or only contractor they'd spoken to. Sixty-three opportunities came through in his first three weeks, in November, his slow season. Exactly one of the 63 questioned him about his minimum. He closed $200,000 in new work in our first 21 days together.

Same ad platform. Same kind of homeowner. The difference was what happened between the click and the handshake. In his words: "These are different leads, guys. I'm meeting respectable folks in nice homes who want to spend the money."

Why "More Leads" Is the Wrong Goal for a Deck Company

You don't need more leads. You need exclusive, referral-quality appointments that show up. Those are different products, and most of this industry sells the first one while contractors are trying to buy the second.

The gap between them is a layer almost nobody in deck marketing talks about: qualification before the form, speed to first contact, confirmation and reminder sequences that get both spouses at the table, no-show recovery, and reactivation for the estimates that stalled last summer. That plumbing is boring and it's where most of the money leaks out. I wrote a whole breakdown on why contractor leads don't pick up the phone if you want the sequences.

One more thing worth understanding about unqualified funnels: every junk opt-in gets counted as a conversion, and the ad algorithm says "got it, this is what a lead looks like" and goes and finds more people exactly like that. That's why cheap, frictionless funnels get worse over time instead of better. Add friction and the algorithm starts learning from serious homeowners. Quality first, then volume follows.

When Buying Deck Leads Is Actually the Right Move

I'm not going to pretend marketplaces are always wrong. If you're a brand-new deck company with no reviews, no referral base and crews sitting idle, go buy leads. You can turn it on today, there's no retainer, and volume, even shared, price-shopped volume, beats an empty calendar when you're starting from zero. Treat every lead like a footrace, collect reviews relentlessly, and plan your exit from day one.

Where it stops working is when you're established and selling $25K to $80K projects. At that ticket, the cost of sitting in a bidding war against four other builders isn't the lead fee. It's the margin you give away to win on price, over and over, plus the jobs you don't get at all. Billy Stewart of Trinity Decks had spent $250,000 on leads over the course of his career through the usual channels before he switched.

How to Buy Deck Leads Without Getting Burned

Whatever you end up choosing, including me, run it through these. Get the answers in writing, not on a sales page.

  1. How many contractors get this same lead? If it's shared, assume you're competing on price before you open your mouth. Get the number per lead in writing.
  2. What's the contact rate? Not the delivery rate. What percentage of these people actually answer? If they've never measured it, they're selling you records, not customers.
  3. What's the cost per sat appointment? The only number that connects to your revenue. A vendor who can't compute it isn't tracking the thing you're buying.
  4. Whose name is on the ad account? If it's theirs, you're renting. Leave and you keep nothing. No pixel history, no audience, no creative.
  5. Is the lead qualified before it reaches me, or after? Ask what questions the homeowner answered before you got the number, and whether budget was one of them.
  6. What happens if they miss? Most guarantees pay out in more of the service that already didn't work. Ask what they owe you, and ask to see the clause.

Deck leads aren't expensive because of the price tag. They're expensive because of the four or five filters between the form fill and a signed contract, and buying on sticker price means you've optimized the one number that doesn't matter. Count contact rate. Count show rate. Count what your windshield time is worth. Then compare.

FAQ

Deck Builder Leads: Common Questions

How much do deck builder leads cost?

It depends entirely on the source. Shared marketplace leads for decking typically run $40 to $150. The deck lead vendors ranking on page one publish rates from roughly $41 to $200 per lead, and $75 to $150 per booked appointment. Search-driven leads in a competitive metro run higher: one San Antonio deck builder I work with was paying roughly $400 a lead for about 15 leads a month before he switched. None of those numbers mean much on their own, because the price on the invoice is not the price per conversation.

Are exclusive deck leads worth more than shared ones?

Usually, yes, and not for the reason most people give. The value isn't that you avoid competition. It's that the homeowner isn't already in a price comparison when you call. A shared lead has been sold to three to five contractors at once, so the conversation starts as a bidding war. Be careful with the word exclusive, though. Plenty of vendors call a lead exclusive because only one company bought that record, while the same homeowner filled out four other forms that week. Ask who else can contact this person, not just who else bought the record.

Why do deck leads stop answering the phone?

Three reasons, in order. The lead was sold to several contractors and the first caller already got them. The form was so frictionless that the person never really committed to anything. Or you called forty minutes later instead of forty seconds later. Contact rate is the number that quietly sets your real cost per lead, and almost nobody measures it. Chip Paynter, a 33-year veteran contractor in California, was reaching about 2 of every 10 leads he bought. After we moved him to an exclusive qualification funnel he was talking with at least 8 of 10.

Should a deck builder buy leads or run their own ads?

If you're brand new with no reviews, no referral base and idle crews, buying marketplace leads is a reasonable first move. It turns on today, there's no retainer, and reps beat an empty calendar. Everyone else should be building an asset instead of renting one. When you run ads inside your own ad account, you keep the pixel history, the audience data and the creative, and the cost curve tends to improve over time rather than get worse.

How many deck leads does it take to book one job?

There's no universal number, and anyone who quotes you one is guessing. What I can tell you is what it looked like for specific builders. Billy Gallegos of Lone Star Home Improvement in Corpus Christi wrote contracts on 3 of the first 7 opportunities the system sent him, roughly $50K in contract value in month one on $50 a day of ad spend. Brian Wallace of Bend Fence & Deck in Oregon sent 60 estimates and closed about 25 of them in six weeks. Your ratio depends far more on your follow-up and your bid process than on the source.

Are Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack worth it for deck builders?

For a brand-new deck company, briefly, with your eyes open and an exit planned from day one. For an established builder selling $25K to $80K projects, the economics usually stop working. You pay per lead whether you reach the person or not, the same record goes to three to five companies, and the credit-dispute process for junk leads becomes a part-time job. Ricardo Cervantes, a contractor I work with, put it this way: "We've used Angie's List, Home Advisor, CraftJack, Thumbtack, and it was always fighting for the customer — whoever was the cheapest would get the job."

What's a realistic cost per booked deck appointment?

Stop tracking cost per lead and start tracking cost per appointment that actually sits down with you. Take a $50 shared lead where 2 of 10 answer and half of those book: that's $500 for one appointment, before your windshield time. The same builders on an exclusive funnel are working from a much higher contact rate, which is why the per-appointment number moves so hard even when the per-lead number looks similar. Ask any vendor for cost per sat appointment, and watch whether they can answer.

Do I own the leads if an agency runs the ads?

Only if you own the ad account. If the agency runs your campaigns from its own Business Manager and bills you monthly, you don't own the pixel history, the audience data or the account. Leave and you start from zero somewhere else. Ask whose name is on the ad account before you ask about anything else, and get the answer in writing.
