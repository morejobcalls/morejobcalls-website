Deck Builder Lead Generation: Every Channel Ranked by Cost Per Booked Appointment

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls

Key Takeaway

Every guide to deck builder lead generation ranks channels by cost per lead, which is the one number that tells you nothing. A channel's real price is what it costs to get a homeowner sitting in a backyard with you, and that number moves by 5x or more depending on how many people answer the phone and how many keep the appointment. Rank your channels on cost per booked appointment and the order changes completely.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I have a stake in the answer, and I'll say so where it matters.

I run Meta ads and appointment systems for deck and outdoor living contractors, so I have an obvious interest in one of the rows below. I'll flag it when we get there. Every client number on this page came out of a real ad account or a recorded call with the owner, and they're named so you can check them.

What Deck Builder Lead Generation Should Actually Be Judged On

Take a $50 shared deck lead. Feels cheap next to a $30,000 job. Now walk it down the chain.

Two of ten answer the phone, because the same record went to four companies and somebody called first. So ten leads, $500, two conversations. Half of those two agree to a time. That's $500 for one appointment, before you've burned a drop of diesel driving to it. And one of the two who booked won't be home when you get there.

Now take a $120 appointment where the homeowner picked the time slot themselves off a calendar and got a text reminder the morning of. The sticker price is more than double. The real price is a quarter.

That's the whole argument. Cost per lead is the number vendors quote because it's the one that makes them look good. The conversions between the lead and the signed contract are where your money actually goes, and almost nobody measures them.

Channel by Channel, and What Each One Is Good For

Referrals, past customers, and the street you're already on

Decks get refinished, extended, railed, screened and roofed over. The homeowner who trusted you with $30,000 four years ago is the easiest yes in your pipeline, and most builders have never once called that list. Same with the four houses that can see your crew working right now. Knock while the joists are up.

The honest tradeoff nobody mentions: referral volume is capped by last year's job count. A slow year produces a slow referral pipeline twelve months later, exactly when you can least afford it. That's the feast-or-famine loop, and asking harder doesn't break it. Chris Walters, who runs E&C Custom Homes in Gulf Shores, Alabama, said it better than I can: "Word of mouth is great, it keeps you busy, but it's never going to take you to the level that you want it to be."

Google Business Profile, reviews, and local search

Free, and it beats most paid channels on cost per booked appointment because the cost is zero and the intent is high. Real project photos, every service listed, reviews asked for out loud at the end of every job. A homeowner searching "deck builder near me" is halfway to a decision already. The limit is volume: only so many people type that phrase in your county this month, and you're splitting them with everyone else in the pack. It's a floor, not a growth plan.

Website SEO

Ranking for decking terms takes three to nine months and then produces work with no per-lead fee, which is the best long-run economics on the list. It's also the channel most likely to get sold to a deck builder who needed appointments last month. If your crews are idle, SEO is not the answer to this quarter's problem. Start it anyway, and solve this quarter somewhere else.

Composite dealer networks

The channel most lists skip. A homeowner who filters a manufacturer's locator down to certified installers in their zip has already picked the material and the budget bracket. Small volume, no dial to turn, but the conversation starts further along than anywhere else. The certification is also worth something in every other channel, because you can put it in an ad and on an estimate.

Paid search and Local Services Ads

Search catches people who already decided to build a deck. That's a real pool and it converts, but it's a small pool, and the per-click prices reflect how many contractors are fishing in it. Local Services Ads publish an average around $53 a lead and shares each one with two or three companies. Both are worth running if you have budget for more than one channel. I compared the two platforms head to head in Google Ads vs Meta for contractors.

Shared marketplaces and pay-per-lead vendors

The honest case: if you're a brand-new deck company with no reviews, no referral base and idle crews, a marketplace is probably your best first move. It turns on today and reps beat an empty calendar.

The problem arrives once you're selling $25K to $80K projects. You pay per record whether you reach the person or not, the same homeowner goes to three to five companies, and disputing junk becomes a part-time job. Ricardo Cervantes, a concrete and outdoor living contractor in Colorado, spent years there: "We've used Angie's List, Home Advisor, CraftJack, Thumbtack, and it was always fighting for the customer — whoever was the cheapest would get the job." I laid out the alternatives in the honest comparison of Angi and HomeAdvisor alternatives, and the source-by-source pricing in what a deck lead really costs once you count the no-shows.

The Four Months Most Deck Builders Hand Away

Every deck builder I meet goes quiet in November and comes back in March, at the exact moment every competitor in the market is bidding for the same attention.

One of my clients ran straight through. By February 25, the deadest stretch of the calendar, he had roughly $300K cash collected, $200K of work on the street and over $700K signed. About $1.2M on the books before March, off roughly $10K in ad spend. The biggest competitor in his market, a guy claiming $6 to $7M a year with seven crews, told him he was struggling to keep his guys busy. Chip Paynter does the same thing on a smaller scale, booking work in November for six to eight months out.

Homeowners plan decks in winter and build them in spring. The builder who's in the conversation in January holds the signed contract in April. There's a fuller version in the deck builder who put $1.2M on the books by February 25.

Know What You'll Do When It Works

This is the part I wish more people warned builders about, because it's where the good outcomes turn stressful.

Chris Walters was running 13 to 14 sales appointments a week and booked out 10 weeks inside two months. Around August 19 we shut his ads off on purpose so he could hire three guys. I get paid to run ads, so that call cost me. It was still right, because past 10 weeks every extra homeowner he booked was a homeowner he'd have to disappoint.

He isn't the only one. Billy Stewart at Trinity Decks was booked 14 to 16 weeks with 15 appointments still to run. Jacob Weaver of Oasis Custom Decks in Pennsylvania had crews booked to September by April. Brian Wallace at Bend Fence & Deck was running five, six, sometimes seven appointments a day.

So before you turn any channel up, answer three questions honestly. Who runs the estimates when there are 13 a week? Who answers the phone at 6pm? What happens if this works in 24 hours instead of 90 days? If the answer to all three is "me, between job sites," fix that first.

And if the plan is to hand one of these channels to somebody else to run, score them before you sign. I keep a scorecard for choosing who runs a deck company's marketing that covers exclusivity, ad account ownership and what a guarantee has to say to be worth anything.

FAQ

Deck Builder Lead Generation: Common Questions

What is the best lead generation for deck builders?

Ranked on cost per booked appointment, the order is usually: past customers and referrals, the neighbors around a job you are already building, your Google Business Profile, your own ad account pointed at a calendar, then everything you rent. The first three cost nothing but are capped by how much work you did last year. The fourth is the first one that scales on demand, which is why most established deck builders end up there. The bottom of the list is shared leads, and it is at the bottom because you pay whether you reach the person or not.

How much does deck builder lead generation cost?

The published per-lead rates run roughly $20 to $110 on shared home-improvement marketplaces, about $53 average on Google Local Services Ads, $2 to $15 or more per click on Google search ads, and roughly $41 to $200 from the pay-per-lead deck vendors that rank for decking terms. None of those are what the channel costs you. In one deck account I run, Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes spent $5,109.15 over about 60 days, which worked out to about $48 per inquiry, about $122 per completed appointment, and about $426 in ad spend per signed deck job. Those three numbers come from the same spend, and only the last two connect to your revenue.

Are shared deck leads or exclusive appointments better for a deck company?

For a brand-new deck company with no reviews and idle crews, a shared marketplace is a defensible first move because it turns on today. For an established builder selling $25K to $80K projects, the economics usually stop working, because the same record goes to three to five companies and the conversation starts on price. Ricardo Cervantes, a concrete and outdoor-living contractor in Colorado, spent years on those platforms and put it this way: "We've used Angie's List, Home Advisor, CraftJack, Thumbtack, and it was always fighting for the customer — whoever was the cheapest would get the job."

How long does deck builder lead generation take to produce jobs?

It depends entirely on which channel you turn on. Referrals and past customers can produce a call this week. Paid demand produces appointments in days: Derek Lopez of Highline Outdoor Living had two appointments booked within six hours of launch, and Josh Throldahl of Throldahl Construction went on 10 sales appointments in his first week, four of them in a single day. SEO and a Google Business Profile take months before they produce anything and then keep producing for years. That difference in timing is the real reason to run more than one channel.

Should a deck builder do SEO or paid ads first?

If your crews are idle right now, paid, because SEO will not fill next month. If your calendar is full and you are thinking about the next three years, SEO, because it compounds and has no per-lead fee once it lands. The honest version is that they answer different questions. Search catches homeowners who already decided to build a deck. Paid social creates the decision in people who were only thinking about it. A deck is a want purchase, not an emergency, so the pool of people who have not searched yet is much larger than the pool who have.

Do composite decking manufacturer programs generate real deck leads?

They generate a small number of unusually good ones. A homeowner who filters a manufacturer's contractor locator down to certified installers in their zip code has already chosen the material and the budget bracket, so the conversation starts further along than almost any other channel. The catch is volume. You cannot turn the dial up, and you cannot plan a year around it. Treat a certification as something that raises your close rate on every other channel too, because it is a credential you can put in an ad and on an estimate.

How many deck leads does it take to book one job?

There is no universal number, and the spread between builders is enormous. Brian Wallace at Bend Fence & Deck sent 60 estimates in his first six weeks and closed about 25, roughly two estimates per job. Billy Stewart at Trinity Decks said he was closing "like 80-something percent" of his appointments 17 days in. A builder who measures, drives home, and emails a bid three days later is usually closer to six or seven estimates per job. Same homeowners, same decks. The ratio is set by your process far more than by the channel.

Can a deck builder generate leads in the winter?

It is the cheapest window on the calendar, because most of your competition has gone quiet and stopped bidding against you. One deck builder I work with ran straight through and by February 25 had roughly $300K cash collected, $200K of work on the street and over $700K signed, about $1.2M on the books before March, off roughly $10K in ad spend. Chip Paynter books work in November for six to eight months out. Homeowners plan decks in winter and build them in spring, so whoever is in the conversation in January holds the contract in April.
