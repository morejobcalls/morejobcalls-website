How a Small-Market Deck Builder Landed a $200,000 Job (After Everyone Told Him Not To)

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
August 25, 2026
Key Takeaway

Josiah Walker of Western Rockies Construction went from advertising in a free local newspaper with one job left on the books to 40–50 opportunities a month — in a market of about 90,000 people spread across eight cities, saturated with contractors. His crew went from two people to six plus a second sub crew, and he's booked through winter and into next spring.

His cousin told him not to do it. Other contractors he trusted called it a scam. He called it a hail mary and did it anyway.

Most case studies start with the win. This one starts with a man who had one job left.

Josiah runs Western Rockies Construction in Montana's Flathead Valley. He's a veteran, he's been out on his own about two and a half years, and when we first spoke he had exactly one job on the books after the one his crew was standing on. Not a slow quarter. One job.

The Before-State: A Free Newspaper and Two Guys

His lead source, in his words: "I was running ads through a free local newspaper. I was paying for the ads, but the newspaper's free."

It was him and one other guy. He knew he needed to get out and run more consultations, but every hour he spent selling was an hour his one employee worked alone. The employee wanted him to hire a third. Josiah didn't think he could afford it — "I don't have it in the budget, I'm too small" — and hired the guy anyway.

They made it through one winter on a single 226-foot deck that took three of them two months. That job was the only reason the business survived the cold. And the whole summer before it, he was doing the math in his head: "The entire summer I was stressed the whole time, knowing winter was coming."

Then the deck ended, and he was back to one job left. Not just his family on the line — two other families depending on the payroll.

The Resistance: Everyone He Trusted Said No

This is the part I want on the record, because it's the part most testimonials sand off.

Josiah didn't come to me curious. He came to me cornered, and then he did everything he could to talk himself out of it.

  1. His cousin, who owns a roofing company, told him flatly not to. He'd been burned by lead-gen companies before, and his advice was one sentence: "Don't do it."
  2. Other contractors he knew and trusted said the same. "Don't do it — it's nothing but a scam."
  3. He didn't believe the channel could work where he lives. His read on his own market: "Both technology-wise and just in general lifestyle-wise, 10 years behind the rest of the US. So when you're like, oh, I do meta ads and Facebook ads — that's not going to work up here."
  4. He ran background research on me I never knew about. "I'm a very skeptical person, so I do my research. I did very extensive research on you that you don't even know about." He went through my past clients' testimonials on his own.
  5. He prayed about it and still felt sick. "Prayed about it a lot, still felt very uneasy and nervous about it." He described the stakes as "damning" if it went wrong.

And then he did it anyway, and he was honest about why: "Let's throw a hail mary and see if he's legitimate or not."

That's not a glowing endorsement. That's a man with one job left deciding the downside of doing nothing was worse. I'd rather have that guy than someone who believes everything he reads.

Why He Actually Did It: The Guys on His Crew

When I asked what tipped him, he didn't talk about ROI. He talked about the people on his payroll.

"Not just as a veteran, but as a person in general — whoever's in my corner, I will fight tooth and nail for. I will literally drain my bank account and suffer on the back end to try and make sure that my guys' families and them are taken care of. I'll put my business under before I let them and their families go under."

He spent money he wasn't sure he had because two other households were counting on the schedule staying full. That's the actual decision most contractors are making when they call me, whether or not they say it out loud.

What Happened: The Numbers

It took about two weeks before anything moved. Then it moved hard: "Once they started coming in, it was more than I could handle."

By April he was already booking into fall. By peak summer, his winter was full and he was booking into the following spring — while the contractors around him were still trying to fill their summer schedules in the middle of peak season.

The $200,000 Deck

The job that best explains the shift wasn't a volume story. It was one homeowner.

They saw the ad, did their own research, and came in already decided. Josiah's account of it: "They're like, I don't care what it costs, this is our guy." Then the line that matters — "I would have never gotten in front of them by a tiny little newspaper ad."

That job is going to come in over $200,000.

He's clear about why it happened, and it isn't the targeting. It's that homeowners see a real person before they ever call: "Wow, we saw your ad, it looked really crisp and clean, super professional. We really liked what you were saying, so we looked you up… and we're like, okay, this is our guy, we've got to call him."

The Part I Didn't Expect: Costco

The metric Josiah brought up unprompted wasn't a number at all.

"When we're walking around Costco and people are recognising my hat — 'Oh, Western Rockies, is that your guys' company? I know we're going to call you guys.' We're actually becoming a brand that people are recognising, not just another contractor that's out there."

In a small market, that compounds in a way it never does in a metro. Eight towns, 90,000 people, and the deck guy from the ads is now the deck guy at Costco.

The Tell: His Competitors Started Copying Him

When Josiah first ran ads, local contractors showed up in his comments to mock him. "A Facebook ad up here? You're clearly not actually from here, you're some Californian." He's born and raised in the valley. He deleted the comments and kept going.

Now the same people are running Facebook ads. Some of them are running his words.

"Now the reverse is I'm noticing a bunch of other contractors now putting on Facebook ads. And not only that, they're starting to copy some of the same wording. So you're first dogging me, but now you're seeing how successful I'm being and you're trying to catch up and copycat."

If you want a signal that a channel works in a market everyone swears it can't work in, that's the one. The skeptics became imitators inside a year.

What Josiah Would Tell You

I asked him what he'd say to the builder sitting where he sat — nervous, small market, burned before, sure it wouldn't work.

"I know it's scary. And I know you probably think it's not going to work in my market. I was in the same boat. But just like when you opened your business — take that leap of faith."

Then he said the thing I'd never ask a client to say:

"I stand very heavily with what I'm willing to put my name on and what I'm not. And I will happily put my name on you and what you do."

The Bottom Line

Josiah is the hardest kind of client to win and the best kind to have. He had no money to gamble, no belief in the channel, and a roster of people he trusts telling him it was a scam. He moved anyway because the people on his crew needed him to.

Eight months later he's got six guys, a sub crew, a $200,000 deck, a calendar full into next spring, and competitors copying his ad copy. His verdict on the risk he took: "Not only did you provide — you over-provided."

If you're where he was, the offer that got him is the same one on the table now: 100 exclusive sales appointment opportunities in 100 days or less — or we write you a check for $10,000.
