Outdoor Living Contractor Marketing: What Actually Books the Big Backyard Jobs

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Outdoor living contractor marketing works when you market each job type the way it's actually bought: searched-for work (repairs, replacements, urgent shade) through Google, and want-purchases nobody searches for yet (new decks, louvered pergolas, outdoor kitchens, pools) through visual ads that qualify the homeowner and book them straight onto your calendar. One generic "outdoor living" ad pulls in kit shoppers and tire-kickers for every job type at once.

Judge every channel on the same number: what it costs to produce an appointment you actually sat. On a $15K to $150K ticket, that number reorders almost every list of marketing tips you'll read.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I have a stake in how this page comes out, and I'll say so when I get to the part that's what we sell. Everything else is either how the channels work or a number out of a real contractor's account, with the contractor named and linked so you can check it.

Most outdoor living contractor marketing advice treats "outdoor living" as one trade. It isn't. A patio cover company in Phoenix, a louvered pergola dealer in Dallas and a pool builder in Tampa sell to roughly the same homeowner, but the homeowner behaves completely differently for each one. If your marketing doesn't account for that, you end up paying for a lot of conversations with people who were never going to buy what you build.

Why Outdoor Living Is Harder to Market Than Most Home Services

Three things make this trade different from roofing, plumbing or HVAC.

It's a want purchase. Nobody's backyard is leaking. There's no emergency forcing a decision this week, so the homeowner can think about it for a season, or three. That means the channel that catches urgent demand (search) sees a lot less of your best work than you'd expect.

The price range inside one category is enormous. A "patio cover" is a $4K kit to one homeowner and a $35K engineered louvered system to another. A "pergola" is a $3,000 big-box kit or a $50K motorized roof. Every lead source that files you under a generic category hands you both buyers and makes you sort them at the kitchen table. I wrote about this squeeze on the patio cover marketing page, and it's the single biggest waste I see in this trade.

The homeowner buys with their eyes. Nobody signs for a $60K backyard off a paragraph of text. They need to see finished work that looks like the yard they want. That's why photos, video and reviews do more of the selling here than in almost any other trade.

Put those together and the job of outdoor living marketing is less "get found" and more "show the right homeowner the right project, screen out the kit shoppers, and get a real appointment on the calendar before they cool off."

Decks

Decks are the most searched job on the list and still mostly a want purchase. Repairs and rotted-board replacements get searched. New composite builds usually don't, until the homeowner has already seen one they like. That's why the deck companies I work with lean on visual ads for new builds. Justin W., a deck and patio builder in San Antonio, runs an outdoor living mix that includes patio and shade projects, and his cost per inquiry went from about $400 to under $30 inside 14 days after six other marketing companies. His line on the first week: "The first week I put out almost a million dollars in quotes. It was crazy. I was hitting jobs that were $100,000, $200,000, $60,000, $80,000." The full picture for this job type lives on the deck builder marketing page.

Patio covers

Patio cover lead generation has one job the others don't: separating the engineered buyer from the kit buyer before either reaches you. Ask project type, budget range, timeline and ownership on the form. Show structural, engineered work in the ad. And answer fast, because a shade inquiry in 105-degree heat is a today phone call, not a Thursday callback. More on that in how we approach patio cover marketing.

Louvered pergolas

This is the job type where search-first marketing hurts the most. A homeowner who would happily spend $50K on a motorized louvered roof often searches nothing, because they've never seen one. You can't win a search war when nobody's searching. A 15-second clip of louvers closing against the rain does the educating your showroom does, every day, in every affluent zip code you serve. I'll be straight with you: we don't have a published, pergola-dealer-specific case study yet, and I say so on the louvered pergola marketing page.

Pools

Pools have the widest gap between who clicks and who can buy. Your real customer is signing for $60K to $150K or more, and they sit in the same inbox as someone pricing a $5,000 above-ground kit. Budget qualification has to happen before booking, and nurture matters more here than anywhere else, because months can pass between first click and contract. Same caveat as pergolas: no named pool-builder case study published yet. The reasoning is on the pool builder marketing page.

Hardscape and outdoor kitchens

The hardscape problem is design time. A homeowner with a Pinterest board and a $3,000 budget books a consult, your designer spends two hours sketching, and the conversation dies the moment you say what a paver patio costs. The closest documented result we have is Ricardo C., a concrete and outdoor-living contractor in Colorado, who closed $105,000 in new jobs from his first seven days on less than $1,000 of ad spend, then put $180,000 in new bids out on the next campaign. The landscaping and hardscape page covers how the budget questions work.

On SEO, the SEO-first agencies are right that it compounds, and you should do it. Build a real page for each job type you sell, keep your Google Business Profile full of fresh project photos, and chase reviews after every install. It's also worth knowing that AI assistants like ChatGPT now answer "who builds patio covers near me," and they pull from the same pages and reviews. Just be honest about what SEO can't do: it can't create demand for a louvered roof nobody knows exists, and it won't save a slow spring you didn't plan for in the fall.

The Number to Run Your Outdoor Living Marketing On

Every channel above will report something. Clicks, impressions, leads, calls. None of those pay your crews. The one number I'd run an outdoor living company on is cost per appointment you actually sat, because it prices in everything the other numbers hide: the phone numbers nobody answers, the no-shows, and the kit shoppers who book and then flinch.

Here's a real example. Chris W., who runs a deck company in Gulf Shores, Alabama, spent $5,109.15 on ads over about 60 days at $100 a day and booked $320,000 in deck jobs, running 13 to 14 sales appointments a week. Divided out, that's about $122 per completed appointment. That's one account over one stretch, not a rate card.

Now do your own math. On a $30,000 average project at a 30% close rate, every sat appointment is worth about $9,000 in expected contract value. At that number, a few hundred dollars to produce one is cheap and $1,200 isn't. On a pool or a louvered roof, the ticket goes up and so does what you can afford to pay. If a channel can't tell you its cost per sat appointment, that's information about the channel.

The Part Nobody Sells: Speed, Follow-Up and Capacity

Here's where I'll flag my bias, because this is what we build. In almost every account I've taken over, the ads weren't the broken thing. The gap between the click and the backyard was.

  • Speed to first contact. An outdoor living inquiry cools off fast, especially in summer. If a human or an automation isn't reaching the homeowner within minutes, you're paying to warm up somebody else's appointment.
  • Automated reminders. Ricardo put it this way: "Every two days I see messages go out: 'Remember that you have an appointment with Ricky this day.' So the follow-up is automatic." That's what keeps a booked appointment from turning into an empty driveway.
  • Nurture for the long-cycle jobs. Pools and louvered roofs don't close in a week. The homeowner who wasn't ready in March might be ready in May, if you're still in front of them.
  • Somebody who can run the appointments. This is the one that surprises owners. When Chris's ads turned on, he told me, "There's probably 40 or 50 that we never even talked to because things were just so busy so quickly." He paused the ads, booked out 10 weeks, and hired. If nobody in your company can run three to five appointments a day, fix that before you spend anything.

Jacob W., a Pennsylvania deck builder, described what happens when all of that works at once: $480,000 closed in 21 days, crews booked until September by April, and his first year over $1 million top line. His description of it: "I'm trying to hold on to this bull that's freaking trying to kick me off of it left and right." A full calendar is a better problem than an empty one. It's still a problem you should plan for.

How to Set Up Outdoor Living Contractor Marketing That Books Jobs

  • Pick your job types on purpose. List what you sell by margin and crew fit. Market the two or three you want more of, not everything on the website.
  • One ad and one set of questions per job type. A patio cover buyer and a pool buyer need different creative and different budget questions, even if the rest of the system is shared.
  • Qualify before the calendar. Project type, budget range, timeline and ownership, asked before anyone can book. Let the kit shoppers screen themselves out.
  • Own the accounts. Ad account, CRM and customer list in your company's name, in writing, before money moves.
  • Plan for the off-season in the fall. The companies with full springs are the ones who were booking in January. Justin's read after a winter on the system: "I've never experienced a January and a February that went this well. Never."
  • Judge on cost per sat appointment for six weeks. Not a bad Tuesday, not a two-week test on $300. Give a channel enough volume to read, then let that number pick the winner.

The Short Version

Outdoor living isn't one trade, so it can't be one ad. Catch the searched-for work with Google and a strong profile, create demand for the big want-purchases with visual ads that qualify and book, and put a real follow-up and appointment layer underneath both. Then run the whole thing on cost per appointment you sat.

FAQ

Outdoor Living Contractor Marketing: Common Questions

What is outdoor living contractor marketing?

It's marketing for companies that build high-ticket backyard projects: decks, patio covers, louvered pergolas, pools, hardscape and outdoor kitchens. What makes it its own category is the buyer. These are want purchases, usually $15,000 to $150,000 or more, that homeowners rarely search for by name and almost always compare on photos, trust and price. So the job isn't just getting found. It's showing the right homeowner a finished backyard, screening out the kit shoppers, and getting a real appointment onto the owner's calendar fast.

What is the best marketing channel for an outdoor living company?

It depends on whether homeowners search for what you sell. Repairs, replacements and anything urgent get searched, so Google search ads, Local Services Ads and your Google Business Profile catch that demand. New decks, louvered roofs, outdoor kitchens and pools mostly don't get searched until the homeowner has already picked a favorite, so a visual channel like Facebook and Instagram ads, pointed at a qualifying application and a booking calendar, creates the demand instead of waiting for it. Most established outdoor living companies end up needing one of each, judged on the same number: cost per appointment you actually sat.

How do patio cover companies get leads without attracting kit shoppers?

Filter before the calendar, not at the kitchen table. Patio cover lead generation goes wrong when the ad and the form are generic, because an engineered $35,000 louvered system and a $4,000 bolt-together kit get lumped under the same words. Ask about project type (solid, lattice, louvered), budget range, timeline and ownership before anyone can book. Show engineered work in the creative, not a generic shade photo. The homeowner anchored at kit pricing should drop out on the form, before they cost you a windshield hour.

Is SEO worth it for outdoor living contractors?

Yes, as a long-term asset, and it's the channel most outdoor living marketing agencies lead with because it compounds. The honest limits are timing and demand. Ranking usually takes months, not weeks, and SEO only captures homeowners who already search, which is thinner for louvered roofs, outdoor kitchens and full backyard builds than for repairs. Do it, keep your Google Business Profile photos and reviews current, and build a page per job type. Just don't make it the only thing standing between you and a slow spring.

Should an outdoor living contractor buy leads from a lead company?

Shared marketplace leads are the weakest fit for this trade, because the same homeowner goes to several companies and a custom backyard turns into a bidding war. Ricardo C., a Colorado concrete and outdoor-living contractor, said it plainly: "We've used Angie's List, Home Advisor, CraftJack, Thumbtack, and it was always fighting for the customer — whoever was the cheapest would get the job." If you pay anyone to produce demand, ask three things in writing: is every inquiry exclusive to you, who owns the ad account and customer data, and what are you actually paying for, a phone number or a sat appointment.

How much should an outdoor living contractor pay per appointment?

Work it back from your average job. On a $30,000 average project at a 30% close rate, a sat appointment is worth about $9,000 in expected contract value, so a few hundred dollars to produce one is cheap. In one deck account I run, Chris W., a Gulf Coast deck builder, spent $5,109.15 over about 60 days, which worked out to about $122 per completed appointment, and he booked $320,000 in deck jobs. That's one account over one stretch, not a rate card, and pools or louvered roofs carry bigger tickets and longer cycles.

Can one marketing system work for decks, patio covers, pergolas and pools?

The engine can, the messaging can't. It's largely the same affluent homeowner in the same zip codes making the same backyard decision, so the targeting, qualifying application, follow-up and booking calendar carry across. What has to change per job type is the creative, the budget questions on the form and how much nurture you run, because a patio cover buyer in 105-degree heat wants a call today and a pool buyer may take months and a financing conversation. Build one system with a separate ad and a separate set of questions per job type.

How do you market louvered pergolas when homeowners don't search for them?

Show them the product before they know to look for it. A homeowner who would happily spend $50,000 on a motorized louvered roof often searches nothing, because they've never seen one. Short video of the louvers closing against rain or sun, run on Facebook and Instagram to homeowners in the right zip codes, does the educating your showroom or home-show booth does, every day. Pair it with a form that names realistic investment ranges so the homeowner thinking of a $3,000 big-box kit filters out before booking.
