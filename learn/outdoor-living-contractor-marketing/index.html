Outdoor Living Contractor Marketing: How to Fill a Calendar With $30K+ Backyard Projects

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Outdoor living contractor marketing has one job: put a homeowner who owns the property, knows the ballpark and wants the project this season on your calendar, exclusively. The buyer for a deck, patio cover, pergola, pool or paver patio is a want buyer, not a need buyer. Most of them never search for the product by name, they cluster in a handful of affluent zip codes, and their timing swings with the seasons.

That rules out most of what gets sold as "marketing" for these trades. Shared marketplaces and manufacturer co-op leads put you in a price fight on a custom product. SEO only catches the minority who search. What works is creating the want with the owner on camera, qualifying before the calendar, and advertising through the off-season while everyone else is dark.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. Decks are the flagship, but the same buyer owns the pool, the louvered pergola and the paver patio, so most of what I run is outdoor living in one form or another. I'll flag where my own system shows up below; everything else is a published rate or a number from a named contractor's ad account.

"Outdoor living" covers a lot of trades: deck builders, patio cover and shade contractors, louvered pergola dealers, pool builders, and design-build hardscape companies. They sell different products and share one customer, and that customer is why generic contractor marketing advice keeps failing them.

Why the Outdoor Living Buyer Breaks Normal Contractor Marketing

A roof leaks and the homeowner buys a roof this week from whoever answers. Nobody's backyard forces them to buy a $45,000 louvered roof. That one difference changes everything downstream.

  • It's a want, not a need. There's no urgency, so speed-to-lead still matters but "first to call" doesn't win the job by itself. Trust does. The homeowner has to believe you're the one who'll build it right, before price ever comes up.
  • Most buyers never search for it. A homeowner who wants a deck might type "deck builder near me." A homeowner who would happily spend $50,000 on a motorized pergola searches nothing, because they've never seen one. You can't win a search war when nobody's searching, which is why SEO-only plans stall for pergola dealers and patio cover companies.
  • The buyer is geographically concentrated. A $30,000 backyard lives in specific zip codes. Broad targeting wastes most of the budget on people who rent, or who own a lot too small for the project.
  • The kit-price anchor. Big-box stores sell a $3,000 aluminum "pergola" and some guy with a trailer bolts together $4,000 kit covers. Half the raw inquiries in these categories arrive anchored to a number that's 10% of your ticket. If your marketing doesn't set the investment range before the appointment, your closer does it in the driveway, and loses.
  • Seasonality cuts both ways. Spring buries you in inquiries you can't get to; by November the phone is quiet and the crews you fought to hire are watching the clock. The contractors who grow are the ones who advertise into the quiet months, and I'll show the numbers below.

The Layer That Decides It: Qualification Before the Calendar

Every outdoor living company that's tried "Facebook ads" and given up has the same story: cheap leads, kit-price shoppers, no-shows. That's not an ads problem. It's a funnel with no friction. A lead form that asks for a name and a phone number tells the ad platform that anyone who'll type a phone number is a conversion, and the platform obligingly finds more of them.

The fix is an application between the ad and the calendar that asks the questions your closer would ask in the first two minutes:

  1. Project type, in the homeowner's words: composite deck, solid or louvered cover, inground pool, paver patio with an outdoor kitchen.
  2. Investment range, with realistic numbers on the buttons. A homeowner picking "$30,000 to $50,000" has anchored themselves before you arrive. A homeowner picking "under $10,000" for a louvered pergola doesn't book, and that's the point.
  3. Timeline: this season, next season, or "just looking," which routes to a nurture sequence instead of your calendar.
  4. Property ownership and, where it matters, whether both decision-makers will be at the appointment.
  5. A date and time, picked by the homeowner, with text and email reminders so they show.

Add that friction and two things happen. Volume drops for a week and the ad platform starts learning from serious homeowners instead of tire-kickers, and within a few weeks the cost per qualified appointment falls while the raw lead count stays lower. Quality first, then volume follows. Chris Walters' account is a clean example: 106 inquiries became 42 completed appointments and 12 signed jobs, and the ads got better at finding the 42 as they ran. Creative: The Owner, the Finished Backyard, and a Number

Three things go in the ad for these trades, and they're the same three regardless of the product.

  • The owner on camera. Not a stock drone shot, not a logo animation. The person who'll show up to the estimate, talking about the project like a builder talks. This is what moves the trust question earlier, and it's the single biggest difference between accounts that work and accounts that don't.
  • The finished backyard, in the homeowner's neighborhood. Real jobs, real addresses' worth of context. For pergolas and patio covers this is also the education: most viewers are seeing a louvered roof for the first time.
  • An investment range, said out loud. "Most of the covers we build run $25,000 to $45,000." It costs you the kit shoppers, which is the point, and it tells the ad platform who to find more of.

Seasonality: Advertise Into the Quiet Months

Outdoor living has a demand curve with a sharp peak, and most contractors market in step with it: heavy in April, dark in December. That's backwards. Ad costs are lowest and the competition thinnest in late fall and winter, while the homeowner who wants the pool or the deck done by Memorial Day is already planning in January. One deck builder client put $1.2 million on the books by February 25 on roughly $10,000 of ad spend, while the other builders in his market were waiting for spring. The full plan is in the $1.2M slow-season breakdown, and it applies to every trade on this page: pools and pergolas have longer lead times than decks, which makes the winter window even more valuable.

The corollary is capacity. Chris Walters ran 13 to 14 sales appointments a week, had 40 to 50 inquiries he never got to call back, got booked out 10 weeks and paused the ads to hire. If you can't run three to five appointments a day, fix that before you turn on demand. The capacity checklist is in the $320K in 60 days case.

FAQ

Outdoor Living Contractor Marketing: Common Questions

What is outdoor living contractor marketing?

Marketing built for companies that sell backyard projects in the $15,000 to $150,000 range: decks, patio covers, louvered pergolas, pools, outdoor kitchens and paver hardscape. It differs from general contractor marketing because the purchase is a want rather than a need, most buyers never search for the product by name, the buyer is concentrated in specific affluent zip codes, and demand swings hard with the seasons. The channels that work are the ones that create demand and qualify it before it reaches your calendar, rather than the ones that resell whoever happened to search.

What is the best marketing for an outdoor living company?

For an established company selling $25,000-plus projects, the channel that produces the most booked appointments per dollar is usually your own Meta ad account, with the owner on camera, pointed at a qualifying application and a booking calendar, backed by referrals and neighbor marketing around active jobs. Shared marketplaces and manufacturer co-op leads put you in a bidding war on a custom product; SEO takes six to twelve months and only catches the minority of buyers who search; home shows work but can't be scheduled. In one deck and outdoor living account, Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes booked about $320,000 in jobs on $5,109.15 of ad spend over roughly 60 days.

How do outdoor living contractors get leads without Angi or HomeAdvisor?

By owning the demand instead of renting it: run ads inside your own account that show the finished backyard to homeowners in the right zip codes, send them to an application that asks project type, budget range, timeline and property ownership, and book the qualified ones straight onto a calendar with automatic reminders. That produces exclusive appointments rather than shared records. Ricardo Cervantes, a Colorado concrete and deck contractor who had used Angi, HomeAdvisor, CraftJack and Thumbtack, closed $105,000 in jobs from his first seven days of appointments on under $1,000 of ad spend after switching.

Do Facebook ads work for patio cover and pergola companies?

Yes, and they suit these trades better than search does, because a homeowner who would happily spend $50,000 on a motorized louvered roof usually doesn't know the product exists and searches nothing. Education-first video ads create the want, and a qualification funnel that names realistic investment ranges filters out the kit-price shoppers before they book. Eric Engle at Mr. Patio Cover in California runs 65 to 70 qualified appointments a week on this model. The budget floor is roughly $3,000 a month in ad spend, and you need someone who can sit three to five appointments a day.

When should an outdoor living contractor advertise?

Earlier than feels natural. Ad costs are lowest and competition thinnest in late fall and winter, while the homeowner who wants a deck or pool by Memorial Day is already planning in January. One deck builder client put $1.2 million on the books by February 25 on roughly $10,000 of ad spend while competitors in his market were dark. Advertise through the off-season to fill spring, and throttle in summer when the crews are full.

Why do manufacturer co-op leads underperform for pergola and patio cover dealers?

Because the same homeowner inquiry is routed to several dealers in the network, all selling the identical system, so the only lever left is price. You also control nothing: not the ad, not the landing page, not the speed of first contact. Co-op leads are worth taking when they're free, but they can't be the plan. A dealer's own ads in their own ad account produce appointments that belong to one company.

How much should an outdoor living contractor spend on marketing?

Build it from appointments rather than a percentage of revenue: jobs you need this month, divided by your close rate, gives sat appointments needed; multiply by what an appointment costs from your best source. At the roughly $122 per completed appointment in the E&C Custom Homes account, a company needing 17 sat appointments a month is spending about $2,000 in ads. A $3,000 a month ad budget is roughly the floor at which a paid social campaign for a high-ticket trade gets enough data to work, and the one thing to check before spending it is whether you have the capacity to run the appointments it produces.
