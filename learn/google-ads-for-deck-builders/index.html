Google Ads for Deck Builders: What It Really Costs Per Booked Appointment

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

Google Ads works for deck builders, but it is capped by something no agency controls: how many people in your county are searching for a deck this month. Search ads rent existing demand. In a trade where nothing is broken and nobody is in a hurry, that pool is small, and every deck company in your market is bidding on the same few hundred searches.

The number that decides whether it is worth it is not cost per click. It is what you pay for a homeowner who actually sat down with you. Follow the chain all the way through and search ads are usually the right second channel for a deck company, and the right first one only in a few specific situations, listed below.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. I run ad accounts for deck companies every day, so I have a bias and you should know it up front: most of what we run is paid social, not search. I am going to tell you where Google Ads for deck builders genuinely beats what I sell, because you can check the math yourself and I would rather you trust the parts where we agree.

Every deck builder PPC page I read to write this one quotes a cost per click and stops. A click is not a job. A click is not even a phone call. So this page follows the money the whole way down: click, inquiry, appointment that happened, contract signed. That is the only chain that tells you anything.

What Google Ads Actually Does for a Deck Builder

Search advertising does exactly one thing. It puts you in front of somebody who has already decided they want a deck and is looking for who builds it. That is a genuinely valuable person. They are further down the road than anyone you will ever reach on Facebook, and they close faster.

The catch is arithmetic. At any given moment, a small slice of the homeowners in your market who would sign a deck contract this year are actively searching for one. A deck is a want purchase. Nobody's backyard is leaking. The roofers and the HVAC guys have it easier here, because a leak or a dead furnace sends someone to Google that afternoon, and a family that has been talking about a back deck for three summers just keeps talking about it.

So when you turn on search ads, you are competing with every other deck company in your county for a pool of people that is already fixed in size. You cannot grow it. You can only win more of it, and the way you win more of it is by paying more per click than the other guy. That is why deck PPC costs go up every spring and never really come back down.

None of that makes it a bad channel. It makes it a capture channel, and capture channels have a ceiling. If your calendar is empty in April, the question is not "how do I win more of the existing searches," it is "where do the other ninety-something percent of buyers come from." That is the question the system we build for deck companies is pointed at, and it is a different question than this page.

What Deck Builder PPC Costs Once You Follow It Past the Click

Here is the honest problem with every cost-per-click number you will read. The agency pages ranking for this term publish click costs in the range of four to twelve dollars for decking terms. Fine. But a click is the first of four steps, and the drop-off at each step is where the money goes.

I can show you the whole chain on an account I run, so you have something real to compare against. Chris W. and his dad Edward run a deck company on the Alabama Gulf Coast. They were a word-of-mouth company that had never hired anybody. This is paid social, not search, but the shape of the funnel is what matters, because the last two rows are identical no matter which platform the click came from:

Read the second and third rows together. $48 to get somebody to raise their hand. $122 to get one of them sitting at a kitchen table. More than half the people who inquired never sat down with anybody. That gap exists on every channel, and it is the part the cost-per-click conversation never touches.

Now apply it to search. Say you are paying $8 a click and one in ten clickers fills out your form. That is $80 to get an inquiry, before you have spoken to anyone. If the same share of those people sit down as on Chris's account, you are at roughly $200 for a completed appointment. Against a deck job worth tens of thousands, $200 is still a fine number. That is the honest case for search ads, and it is a good one.

What makes it fall apart is volume. If there are only forty deck searches a month in your county and you win a quarter of them, you are looking at ten clicks, maybe one inquiry, and a very quiet calendar. The cost per appointment looked great. It just did not happen often enough to run a business on. Small-market builders get burned by this constantly, and it is the same math that makes SEO disappoint a deck company in a rural county.

Local Services Ads vs Search vs Performance Max for a One-to-Five-Crew Builder

Google will happily sell you three different products here, and they behave nothing alike at the size most deck companies actually are.

ProductWhat you pay forExclusive to you?Fits a small deck company?
Local Services AdsA lead, not a clickNo. SharedYes, if you have review volume and answer the phone live
Search adsA clickNo, but the click is yoursYes, with tight keywords and a real landing page
Performance MaxWhatever the algorithm decidesNoRarely. Needs conversion volume you do not have

Local Services Ads sit at the very top with the green check. You pay per lead instead of per click, which sounds better and sometimes is. The thing to understand is that the lead goes to several contractors, the same way Angi does it, so you are back in a race to call first. If you have a pile of reviews and somebody who picks up the phone on the first ring, it is the cheapest honest place to start on Google. If your phone goes to voicemail at 2pm, do not bother. You will pay for leads that another builder converts.

Search ads are where the control is. You choose the words, you choose the page they land on, you see exactly what you paid for. For a deck company the winning setup is narrow: the handful of terms where somebody is clearly shopping for a builder, a page about that specific thing, and an aggressive negative keyword list to keep out everyone searching how to build a deck themselves. That negative list matters more here than in most trades, because decks have an enormous DIY audience that will happily spend your money.

Performance Max is the one I would leave alone. It needs a steady diet of conversions to learn from, and a deck company producing a handful of inquiries a week never feeds it enough. You end up paying for YouTube and Display impressions while Google searches for a signal that is not there. The channel-by-channel version of this argument, across all contractor trades, is in Google Ads vs Meta Ads for contractors. I am not going to repeat it here.

When Google Ads for Deck Builders Should Be Your First Channel

Four situations. If you are in one of them, start on search and I would tell you the same on a call:

  • You sell a repair or a replacement. Deck repair, board replacement, rotten joists, failed railings. Those people search, because something is actually broken. This is the strongest case for search in this trade and it is the one most deck marketing ignores.
  • You are in a big metro. If there are real search volumes in your county, the pool is big enough to build on. Volume fixes most of my objection.
  • You have review volume and live phone coverage. That combination makes Local Services Ads cheap for you specifically, in a way it is not for a builder with nine reviews.
  • You need something tomorrow and have nothing built. Search turns on fast. So does paid social, but search needs less creative work to get started, and if you are a week from an empty schedule that matters.

And when it should not be first: you are in a small or rural market, you sell big custom builds rather than repairs, you have no reviews yet, or you are trying to grow past what your market is already searching for. In all four of those, search will produce a respectable cost per appointment on a volume too small to matter. Josiah W. builds decks in Montana, in a market of about 90,000 people spread across eight cities and saturated with contractors. Everyone told him marketing would not work there. It was not search that fixed it. He went to 40 to 50 opportunities a month, his crew went from two people to six plus a second sub crew, and he is booked through winter and into next spring.

How to Structure a Deck Builder Google Ads Campaign

If you are running it yourself, the structure below is what I would build. It is deliberately boring.

  1. Split repair from new builds. Different intent, different urgency, different money. Same campaign is how you end up paying custom-build prices for a $900 railing job.
  2. Keep the keyword list short and shopping-flavored. "Deck builders near me," "deck contractor," "composite deck installer," your town plus deck builder. Terms where somebody is looking for a person, not information.
  3. Build the negative list before you launch, not after. DIY, how to, plans, kits, cost calculator, home depot, lowes, jobs, hiring, salary. Add to it weekly from your search terms report. This single step separates accounts that work from accounts that bleed.
  4. Send clicks to a page about the thing they searched. Not your homepage. A composite deck search should land on composite decks, with your photos and one obvious next step.
  5. Make the next step a real offer. "Contact us" is not an offer. A free custom design and estimate is, and it converts at a different rate.
  6. Put a qualifying question or two in front of the calendar. Fewer inquiries, more of them worth driving to.
  7. Track appointments, not form fills. If your reporting stops at conversions, you are optimizing toward the wrong thing.

Run your own account and you own the data and the history, which is worth more than most people realize. Whatever you do, keep the account in your name.

The Part That Decides Whether Any of It Works

You can do everything above correctly and still get nothing out of it, because the expensive failure in this trade happens after the click.

A homeowner fills out your form at 11am. Somebody calls them back the next afternoon. By then they have talked to two other builders, and you are paying for a conversation you did not get to have. Go back to Chris's numbers: 106 inquiries, 42 sat appointments. The 64 that went nowhere cost the same as the 42 that happened. Halving that loss is worth more than any bid adjustment you will ever make, and it costs nothing but discipline. There is more on why that gap opens in why contractor leads don't answer.

The same discipline is what turns a bad account around. Justin W., a deck and patio builder in San Antonio, had hired six other companies before us. He was at roughly $400 per lead at maybe 15 a month. Within 14 days of launch he was at 30 a week at under $30, and the first week he put out almost a million dollars in quotes. His words: "I've had six other companies involved in this space in my life." The platform was not the variable that moved.

So before you spend a dollar on clicks: who calls, how fast, and what happens on call three when they did not answer the first two? If you cannot answer that, search ads will just help you lose money more efficiently.

The Honest Verdict

Run search ads if you sell deck repairs, if you are in a metro with real volume, or if you have the reviews and phone coverage to make Local Services Ads cheap. They capture people who are already looking, and people who are already looking are good people to meet.

Just do not expect them to fill a calendar by themselves in a market where a few dozen people a month are searching. That is not a failure of your campaign. It is the size of the pool. When a deck company needs more appointments than its market is currently searching for, the answer is a channel that creates demand rather than renting it, which is why nearly everything I run for deck builders on paid social is built the way it is. Best case, you run both, and search quietly picks up the repair work while the other channel fills the spring schedule.

Common Questions

Do Google Ads work for deck builders?

Yes, with a ceiling. Search ads put you in front of homeowners who have already decided they want a deck and are looking for a builder, and those people close faster than anyone you reach on paid social. The limit is that a deck is a want purchase, so the number of people searching in your county this month is fixed and usually small. Search ads win you a larger share of that pool. They cannot make it bigger. That makes them an excellent capture channel and a poor rescue plan for an empty spring calendar.

How much do Google Ads cost for a deck builder?

The agency pages ranking for this term publish decking click costs in the range of four to twelve dollars, but the click is the first of four steps and the number that matters is what you pay for a homeowner who actually sat down with you. On an account I run for Chris W., a deck builder in Gulf Shores, Alabama, $5,109.15 of ad spend produced 106 homeowner inquiries, 42 completed appointments and 12 signed jobs worth $320,000. That is about $48 per inquiry, $122 per completed appointment and $426 in ad spend per job won. Work your own numbers down that same chain before you judge a cost per click.

Is Google Ads or Facebook better for a deck company?

They do different jobs. Google reaches the small share of homeowners already shopping for a deck builder. Paid social reaches the much larger group who would sign for a deck this year but have not started looking, because nothing is broken and nothing is forcing them to hurry. If you sell deck repairs, start with search. If you sell big custom builds, or you are in a market too small to have meaningful search volume, demand creation is where the appointments are. Most established deck companies eventually run both.

Should a deck builder use Local Services Ads?

Only if two things are true: you have real review volume, and somebody picks up the phone live during the day. Local Services Ads charge per lead instead of per click and sit above everything else on the page, which is genuinely good. But the lead goes to several contractors at once, the same way a marketplace works, so it turns into a race to call first. A builder with nine reviews and a voicemail greeting will pay for leads that somebody else converts.

Is Performance Max worth it for a deck contractor?

Usually not at the size most deck companies are. Performance Max needs a steady volume of conversions to learn from, and a deck company producing a handful of inquiries a week never feeds it enough signal. What you get instead is spend spread across YouTube and Display while the algorithm hunts for a pattern that is not there. Narrow search campaigns with a hard negative keyword list give a small builder far more control over the same budget.

What negative keywords should a deck builder add?

Build the list before you launch, because decks have a huge DIY audience that will happily spend your budget. Start with DIY, how to, plans, kits, cost calculator, free, cheap, Home Depot, Lowes, jobs, hiring, salary, and any material you do not work with. Then add to it every week from your search terms report. This is the least glamorous step in a deck builder PPC account and it is the one that most often separates the accounts that work from the accounts that bleed.

How long before Google Ads produce deck appointments?

Clicks start the day you turn it on, and inquiries usually follow within the first week. Appointments depend almost entirely on how fast you call people back, not on the platform. The account takes about 30 to 60 days of search term cleanup and bid adjustment to settle into a stable cost per appointment, so judge it on that window rather than on the first fortnight. If you are still getting nothing after 60 days of honest optimization, the problem is normally market size or follow-up speed rather than the campaign.

Can I run Google Ads myself or should I hire someone?

A narrow search campaign with a good negative keyword list is genuinely something an owner can run, and I would rather see you do that than hand a small budget to somebody who will not look at it. The part that is hard to do yourself is not the account, it is the follow-up: calling every inquiry within minutes, all day, in the middle of running crews. Whoever runs it, keep the ad account in your own name so the data and the history stay yours.
