How Much Should a Contractor Pay Per Lead? The 2026 Math, Trade by Trade

Spencer Wright — Founder, More Job Calls
Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

How much should a contractor pay per lead? Less than you think, and it's the wrong question. Published prices run about $40 to $150 per shared marketplace lead, $41 to $200 from pay-per-lead vendors, and $300 to $400 per lead from search ads in a competitive metro. But a lead is a phone number, not a conversation. A $50 lead where two of ten people pick up is a $250 conversation and, if half of those book, a $500 appointment.

The number to buy on is cost per sat appointment, judged against your average job and your close rate. On a $30,000 job at a 30% close, one sat appointment is worth about $9,000 in expected contracts. A few hundred dollars to produce one is cheap. Twelve hundred is not.

MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I sell one of the options on this page, and I'll say so when we get there. Every other number here is either a publicly quoted platform rate or a figure out of a named contractor's ad account, so you can go check it.

"How much should I pay per lead" is the most common question I get from contractors, and I've spent over $10 million on ads for home-service companies trying to answer it properly. The honest answer has three parts: what leads cost right now, what they actually cost once you count who answers, and the ceiling your own business can afford. Most articles stop at the first part.

The Number That Isn't on the Invoice: Contact Rate

Chip Paynter is a 33-year veteran general contractor out of Loomis, California. When we first met he told me the platforms used to work for him, and then slowly stopped: he'd buy ten leads and maybe two would pick up. His line about Yelp stuck with me: "We talked to Yelp 18 different times. Whether we spent $50 a month or $1,500 a month, the leads were about the same either way." Of course they were. A platform is paid when a form is filled, not when you sign a job, so form fills are what it optimises for.

The Ceiling: What Your Business Can Actually Afford Per Appointment

Here's the calculation I walk every contractor through on a first call. It needs three numbers you already have: your average job value, your close rate on appointments you actually sat, and your gross margin. Then:

  1. Expected contract value of one sat appointment = average job × close rate.
  2. Expected gross profit of one sat appointment = that × gross margin.
  3. A comfortable ceiling is around 10% of the expected contract value, which keeps marketing well inside the margin even on a bad month. Anything under that and volume, not price, is your problem.

How to Set a Marketing Budget From This

Skip the percentage-of-revenue rule of thumb and build it from appointments:

  1. Revenue you want this month ÷ average job = jobs needed.
  2. Jobs needed ÷ close rate = sat appointments needed.
  3. Sat appointments needed × cost per sat appointment from your best source = the budget.

A deck company wanting $150,000 a month at a $30,000 average job needs five jobs, which at a 30% close is about 17 sat appointments. At the $122 per completed appointment in the E&C account that's roughly $2,000 in ad spend; at the $500 real cost of a shared lead it's $8,500, before the margin you give away to win the bid. That gap is the entire reason to measure this properly. And before you buy anything, count how many of last year's estimates you actually closed, because close rate moves this number more than any lead price does. That's the first chapter of how to get more deck jobs without buying more leads.

FAQ

How Much Should a Contractor Pay Per Lead: Common Questions

How much should a contractor pay per lead in 2026?

Judge it against the job, not the invoice. Shared marketplace leads for home improvement commonly run $40 to $150 each, pay-per-lead vendors publish roughly $41 to $200 per lead, and search ads in a competitive metro can cost $300 to $400 per lead. None of those numbers tells you what you can afford. Work it backwards: average job value times close rate gives you the expected contract value of one sat appointment, and a sensible ceiling is a small slice of that. On a $30,000 deck at a 30% close, an appointment is worth about $9,000 in expected contracts, so a few hundred dollars per sat appointment is cheap and $1,200 is not.

What is a good cost per lead for contractors?

There is no good cost per lead on its own, because a lead is a phone number, not a conversation. A $50 lead where two of ten people answer is a $250 conversation, and if half of those book it is a $500 appointment. A $48 inquiry on an exclusive funnel where eight of ten answer is a very different product at nearly the same sticker price. Compare sources on cost per sat appointment and, if you can get it, ad spend per signed job.

How much do Angi and HomeAdvisor leads cost for contractors?

Angi Leads, which absorbed HomeAdvisor, prices by trade and market, and home-improvement categories commonly land in the $40 to $150 per lead range, with bigger-ticket trades at the top of it. The price is only half the cost. The same homeowner's request goes to three to five contractors at once, so you pay whether or not they answer, and when they do answer you are one of several bids on the same project.

What does a booked appointment cost from Facebook ads for a contractor?

It depends on the trade, the market and the offer, so treat any single figure as one account over one stretch rather than a rate. In one deck account, Chris Walters at E&C Custom Homes in Gulf Shores, Alabama spent $5,109.15 over about 60 days for 106 inquiries, 42 completed appointments and 12 signed jobs, which works out to about $48 per inquiry, $122 per appointment and $426 in ad spend per job. Derek Lopez at Highline Outdoor Living ran 37 sales appointments in 30 days on $1,295, about $35 each.

Should a contractor pay per lead or per appointment?

Per appointment, if you can get it and if the appointment is exclusive. Paying per lead means you carry the contact-rate risk: every number that never picks up is money gone. Paying for exclusive booked appointments moves that risk to whoever is producing them. The catch with most pay-per-appointment vendors is that you rent the pipeline, so the day you stop paying it stops, and you kept no ad account, audience or creative. Owning the ad account and paying for management usually costs more up front and less per appointment over a season.

What percentage of revenue should a contractor spend on marketing?

Most home-service contractors land somewhere between 5% and 10% of revenue on marketing when they are actively growing, and less when they are booked out. The more useful way to set the number is per appointment: decide how many sat appointments you need this month to hit your revenue target at your close rate, multiply by what an appointment costs you from your best source, and that is the budget. A $3,000 a month ad budget is roughly the floor at which a paid social campaign for a high-ticket trade gets enough data to work.

Why did my cost per lead go up even though nothing changed?

On shared marketplaces, because more contractors joined your category and the platform is paid per form fill, not per job, so your spend rises and the quality does not. On your own ad account, usually because an unqualified funnel taught the algorithm that junk opt-ins are conversions and it went and found more of them. The fix on your own account is to add qualifying questions before the calendar so the algorithm optimises toward serious homeowners; quality comes first, then volume follows.
