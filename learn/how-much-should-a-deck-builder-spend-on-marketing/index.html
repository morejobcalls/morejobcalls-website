MoreJobCalls is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. "How much should a deck builder spend on marketing" comes up on almost every first call I take, and the honest answer is that the percentage everyone quotes is a guess, while the number you can actually build a budget from is sitting in your own ad account and your own close rate. How Much Should a Deck Builder Spend on Marketing? The Percentage Rule First

The figure most often repeated for home-service contractors is somewhere between 5% and 10% of revenue while you're actively growing, and less once you're booked out. It's a rule of thumb, not a finding, and I'd treat every version of it the same way: useful for checking whether a number is absurd, useless for deciding what the number should be. A $1.5M deck company running that rule lands anywhere from $75,000 to $150,000 a year, and a range that wide isn't a budget.

Three problems with it, specific to decks. First, it starts from last year's revenue, and a marketing budget is supposed to buy next year's. Second, a deck company's revenue arrives in lumps of $25,000 to $80,000, so a month that is 5% of revenue and a month that is 15% can be the same campaign with one contract signed a week apart. Third, it says nothing about what the money buys. Ten percent of revenue on shared leads and 2% on your own ad account can produce the same number of signed decks, and the percentage rule can't tell those two apart.

The Better Method: Build the Deck Builder Marketing Budget From Appointments

This is the calculation I walk every contractor through on a first call, and it's the same one I used for all trades in how much a contractor should pay per lead. It needs three numbers you already have or can count from last year's estimates.

  1. Revenue you want ÷ average job = jobs needed.
  2. Jobs needed ÷ close rate on sat appointments = sat appointments needed.
  3. Sat appointments needed × cost per sat appointment from your best source = ad budget.

Then two checks. Roughly $3,000 a month in ad spend is the floor at which a paid social campaign for a high-ticket trade gets enough data to work, so if step three lands under it, the floor is your number. And appointments have to be sat by someone and installed by someone, so if step two produces more appointments than your crews can build, capacity is your number, and the budget goes down, not up.

The $500 column is what a $50 shared lead really costs per appointment once two of ten homeowners answer and half of those book, the same arithmetic I laid out on the cost-per-lead page.

Three things fall out of that table. The appointment method lands well under the percentage rule at every size, if the appointments are exclusive. For the two smaller companies the floor sets the budget, not the math, and at roughly $122 per sat appointment a $3,000 month produces something like 24 sat appointments, which is three times what a $750K company needs. That surplus is why, for a smaller company, this becomes a capacity question, and it's exactly what happened to Chris Walters, who ran about $100 a day and was booked out 10 weeks inside two months. And the $500 column is the price of buying on sticker: the same appointments cost two to four times as much before you count the margin you give away winning a shared bid.

Those figures are ad spend only; management and tools sit on top, and I'll get to each. Ad spend is the line that moves with appointments needed, so it's the one to build the budget around.

Seasonality: Spend More in Winter, Not Less

Most deck builders spend the way revenue arrives: heavy in spring, quiet in winter. That's backwards.

Nobody builds a deck in January. But a $40,000 to $100,000 outdoor living project gets planned for weeks or months, and a lot of that planning happens in winter. Meanwhile almost every competitor has paused, so there are fewer builders bidding for the same homeowners, and a January appointment is a spring build. The pillar goes through why winter is the cheapest, least-contested window on a deck builder's calendar; the case that proves it is a client who had roughly $300K cash collected, $200K of work on the street and over $700K signed by February 25, about $1.2M for the year, off roughly $10K in ad spend. I break that down in the deck builder who closed $1.2M in the slow season. Justin Wylie's version, in his words: "I've never experienced a January and a February that went this well."

So if your budget has to be uneven, make it uneven the other way. Hold the floor year-round, then put the extra into the months your competitors go quiet, and let spring be the season you're installing what winter booked. The one caveat is capacity, which is the next section: winter appointments are spring installs, and you need to know how far out you can book before you fill the calendar with promises.

When Not to Spend: Capacity Is the Ceiling

There's a point where the right marketing budget is zero for a while, and it comes sooner than most owners expect. Chris Walters hit it in about 60 days. Running about $100 a day, he was doing 13 to 14 sales appointments every week, and by the middle of August he was booked out 10 weeks. Around August 19 we shut the ads off on purpose while he hired three more guys. His words: "There's probably 40 or 50 that we never even talked to because things were just so busy so quickly." Those homeowners raised their hands for a deck estimate and never got a call back, and every one of them now thinks E&C doesn't return calls. That is the cost of running ads past your capacity, and no percentage rule will warn you about it.

Before the budget goes on, and before it goes back on, have answers to these:

  • Who runs the appointments when there are 13 a week? If the answer is "me, between job sites," someone else has to own the calendar first.
  • How far out can you install before homeowners walk? Ten weeks worked for Chris in a strong market. Know your number before you hit it, because hitting it is the signal to hire or pause.
  • Can your crews absorb a second one? If you have a bench or a sub you trust, you keep the ads on while your competitor is working out payroll.
  • Is every inquiry landing somewhere you'll see it? A CRM with follow-up built in, not an inbox. The volume arrives all at once, and the first month is where the money leaks.

Turning the budget off when the calendar is full is the campaign working, not failing. When Chris's new crews are producing, the budget goes back on, and now he knows exactly what $100 a day does to his phone.

Putting the Deck Builder Marketing Budget Together

So: how much should a deck builder spend on marketing? Start with the appointment math. Count last year's estimates and how many you closed, pick the revenue you actually want, and work out how many sat appointments that takes at your close rate. Price those appointments from your best source, which for an established deck company selling five-figure projects is almost always your own exclusive ad account rather than bought leads; what deck builder leads really cost shows why. Hold the roughly $3,000 a month floor in ad spend, keep management and tools as separate lines, put the extra into winter, and write down the install lead time that tells you to pause.

Then check the answer against 5% to 10% of revenue, and don't be surprised when it comes in under. The named accounts on this page ran between roughly 0.6% and 2.8% of contract value in ad spend. That gap is the whole argument for building the budget from appointments instead of from a percentage: one method tells you what's normal, the other tells you what's true for your company.

Should a deck builder spend more on marketing in winter or in summer?
