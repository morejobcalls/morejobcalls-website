Choosing an Agency

How to Choose a Contractor Marketing Agency

Compare every provider on one number: what a sales appointment that actually sits costs you, all in. Not cost per lead or cost per click, and not where you rank in Google. Push each quote all the way through to a homeowner sitting at a kitchen table with your estimate in front of them, then compare.

I've read most of the "best contractor marketing agency" roundups, and they share one hole. The agency that published the list sits at the top of it, no page prints a cost per appointment, and "qualified lead" never gets defined. So contractors end up deciding on tone of voice instead of arithmetic. The arithmetic is below.

That last column is where most of these conversations end. An agency that won't put a number in writing is asking you to fund its learning curve, and you've probably already paid for one of those.

Here's a real account instead of a promise. Chris W., a custom deck and home builder in Gulf Shores, AL, spent $5,109.15 on ads across about 2 months at about $100 a day, which produced 106 inquiries, 42 booked appointments and 12 signed jobs, so his ad spend came out around $122 per booked appointment and around $426 per signed job. He booked $320,000 worth of jobs in those first two months, got booked out 10 weeks and paused the ads to hire. Those figures are ad spend only, so add whatever a provider charges on top before you compare anybody. Then ask every agency you call for that same chain on an account they run right now: ad spend, then inquiries, then appointments booked, then jobs signed. Most can only hand you the first two, which tells you where their work stops.

The gap between an inquiry and a job is almost never the ads. It's who calls in the first ten minutes, whether both decision makers were confirmed before the slot got booked, and who rescues the no-show. Nobody puts that layer in a proposal, so it quietly stays on your plate. Why your leads don't pick up and speed to lead for contractors cover what it costs you when that layer is missing.

FAQ

Contractor Marketing Agency Questions

What does a contractor marketing agency actually do?

Most of them run traffic. They build or rebuild your website, manage ad campaigns, work on rankings and send you a monthly report on clicks, impressions and leads. What happens after a homeowner fills in the form is usually left with you: the calling, the qualifying, the booking, the confirming and the no-show rescue. That's the part that decides whether any of it turns into signed work, so it's worth asking who owns it before you sign anything.

How do I choose a contractor marketing agency?

Make every provider quote you the same number: the cost of one sales appointment that actually gets sat, on an account they run right now. Then ask for the chain behind it, which is ad spend, inquiries, appointments booked and jobs signed. An agency that can give you the first two and not the last two is selling you traffic and calling it growth. Also get in writing who owns the ad account and the CRM data if you leave, and what the agency owes you if the numbers don't show up.

What's the difference between a contractor marketing agency and a lead generation company?

A lead generation company sells you contact details, and the same homeowner is often sold to several companies at once. An agency manages marketing work on your behalf and bills you whether the phone rings or not. Neither one books the appointment for you. We do a third thing: exclusive sales appointments booked onto your calendar, in your own ad account, with the qualifying and the follow-up done before the slot lands.

Does any contractor marketing agency guarantee appointments?

Almost none do, and that's the fastest filter you have. Ours is 100 exclusive appointment opportunities in 100 days, or you get paid three ways: a 100% refund, a $2,000 check from us, and we keep working for free until we hit 100. It's in writing. Full terms at apply.morejobcalls.com/terms.

Is a contractor marketing agency worth it if I already buy leads?

Work out what you're paying per appointment that sits, not per lead, and the answer usually shows itself. A shared lead is cheap because four companies are buying the same homeowner, and the homeowner stops picking up by the third call. Once you divide your lead spend by the appointments you actually ran, the cheap option tends to stop looking cheap. The Angi arithmetic walks through that math with published prices.
