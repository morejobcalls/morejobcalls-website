Pay Per Appointment Marketing for Contractors: How the Model Actually Works

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
October 21, 2026 · Updated October 2026

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. So I sell something adjacent to what this page is about, and I'll be straight about where the model beats mine.

Key Takeaway

Pay per appointment marketing for contractors means you pay a flat price for each appointment delivered instead of paying per lead or paying a monthly fee. The published rates from vendors that rank for contractor appointment terms run roughly $110 to $150 per appointment. That price is not what decides whether the deal is good.

What decides it is the contract's definition of "appointment": whether the homeowner is exclusive to you or shared with three other contractors, what qualification was actually checked, how far out the slot can be booked, and who eats the no-show. Two vendors charging $125 can be selling completely different products.

Quick disclosure before anything else: I run an appointment system, so I am not a neutral party here. I am going to tell you where pay per appointment is genuinely the right call, because the contractors worth working with are the ones who do this math properly instead of signing with whoever pitches hardest.

Here is the thing almost nobody selling this model will put in front of you. Chris W. and his dad Edward run a deck company on the Alabama Gulf Coast, a word-of-mouth company that had never hired an agency. Over about 60 days on $5,109.15 of Meta ad spend at $100 a day, they logged 106 homeowner inquiries, 89 opportunities in the CRM, 42 completed sales appointments and 12 jobs won, for $320,000 in booked deck work. Divide that same spend three ways and you get about $48 per inquiry, about $122 per completed appointment, and about $426 in ad spend per signed deck job.

Hold onto that $122. It is a completed appointment, exclusive to one deck company, in a real market, with the full funnel visible. Now go back and look at the $110 to $150 the appointment vendors publish, and notice that nobody tells you whether their number is a booked slot or a held one, or how many other contractors got the same homeowner. That is the entire game.

What Pay Per Appointment Marketing for Contractors Actually Means

Strip the pitch off and the model is simple. A vendor spends its own money generating demand, calls the people who respond, gets one of them to agree to a day and time, and drops that appointment on your calendar. You pay a fixed price per appointment. No retainer, often no setup fee, and you are not buying ad spend.

The appeal is obvious and it is real: your downside is capped. You are not writing a check into a month that might not work. For a contractor who has been burned by a retainer that produced nothing, that is worth a lot, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

But "pay per appointment" is not one product. It is at least three, and the difference between them is bigger than the difference in price:

  • Pay per booked. You pay when somebody agrees to a time. Cheapest, and every no-show is yours.
  • Pay per held. You pay when the homeowner is actually there. The vendor now carries no-show risk, which changes how carefully they book.
  • Pay per qualified held. You pay when a homeowner who matches an agreed definition actually sits down with you. This is the only version where your interests and the vendor's are pointed the same direction.

If a contract does not say which of those three you are buying, you are buying the first one.

The Five Things That Have to Be in Writing

An appointment is not a thing in the world. It is whatever your contract says it is. Here are the five definitions to pin down, and what a weak answer sounds like next to a real one.

The exclusivity row is the one that costs contractors the most money, and it is the easiest to skim past. Some appointment programs are generating demand on comparison and review sites where a homeowner is explicitly invited to request quotes from several contractors. That homeowner genuinely agreed to meet you. They also agreed to meet three other people, and the conversation you are driving to is going to open on price. You did not buy an appointment. You bought a seat in a bidding war, at appointment prices.

Brian W. and Jessica M. run a fence and deck company in Bend, Oregon. Before they found me they had paid another marketer for a program that sounded a lot like ours, and got 10 to 15 weak appointments. The appointments existed. They were on the calendar. They were just not with people who were going to buy a fence. That distinction does not show up in a per-appointment price, and it is the whole difference between a cheap appointment and an expensive one.

The Arithmetic to Run Before You Sign

Do not compare prices per appointment. Compare cost per signed job, and be honest about your own close rate. The math is four numbers.

Take the price per appointment. Divide by your show rate, because an appointment nobody attends still got billed. Divide by your close rate on the appointments that do happen. That gives you cost per job. Compare it against your gross profit on an average job, not your revenue.

Worked through, at $125 per booked appointment with a 70% show rate and a 30% close rate, you are at roughly $595 per signed job. On a $30,000 deck that is nothing worth arguing about. The same $125 with a 50% show rate and a 15% close rate is about $1,667 per job, and now you are in a real conversation about whether the appointments are any good.

Notice what moved the answer. The price per appointment did not change. The show rate and the close rate did, and neither of those is controlled by the vendor's price list. That is why I keep pushing people back to the definition instead of the rate. Who Pay Per Appointment Actually Suits

It is a good fit when:

  • Your crews are idle right now and you need something that turns on this week.
  • You have never run paid demand and you want to see whether appointments convert for you before you build anything.
  • Your average job is large enough that even a bad cost per job is survivable. Deck and outdoor living work usually clears this bar.
  • You have a real sales process. The model hands you appointments and nothing else. If quoting takes you a week, you will waste them.

It is a poor fit when:

  • You want to build something that compounds. You are renting, and the meter never stops.
  • You need volume. Per-unit pricing gets expensive fast, and the vendors who can scale volume are usually the ones loosest about qualification.
  • Your market is small enough that one vendor working several contractors in it means you are meeting your own competitors' homeowners.
  • You care who shows up. At that point you want control of the targeting and the creative, and this model does not give you either.

The Question Almost Nobody Asks

What do you have on day one after you stop paying?

With pay per appointment, honestly, nothing. No ad account with a year of conversion data in it. No creative that you can hand to the next person. No pixel history, no audiences, no list. The pipeline stops the same week the invoices do, and you are exactly where you started, except poorer and a year older.

That is not a scandal. It is what renting is. It is the same deal as renting an excavator, and nobody is upset about that. Just price it as renting, and do not confuse it with building something. Set vs. Shown vs. Sat: What Each Appointment Word Means

A set appointment is one a homeowner agreed to. A shown appointment is one where the homeowner was actually there at that time. A sat appointment is one where you met every decision-maker and presented your offer. Every quote you get counts one of those three, and the gap between them is where the money goes.

The wordWhat actually happenedWhat it means for the price
Set (booked)The homeowner picked a day and timeCheapest per unit. Every no-show is yours
Shown (held)The homeowner was home, or on the call, when you got thereThe vendor carries no-show risk, so the price per unit goes up
SatYou sat down with every decision-maker and presented your offerThe only one that can turn into a signed job that day

Pay per show means you're billed only when the homeowner actually shows up. That's a real improvement over pay per set, but a show still isn't a sit. If one spouse is home and the other's at work, the vendor got paid and you got a tour of the backyard.

Run it through the same arithmetic from earlier. At $125 per set appointment and a 70% show rate, each shown appointment really costs you about $179. If only half of the shown ones have every decision-maker at the table, each sat appointment costs about $357, and that's the number to hold up against your gross profit.

The Short Version

Pay per appointment marketing is a legitimate model with a capped downside, and for a contractor who needs appointments this week and does not want to fund ad spend, it is often the right first move. Published rates sit around $110 to $150. Ignore the rate. Get qualification, timing, exclusivity, no-show handling and disputes defined in writing, then run the price through your own show rate and close rate to get a cost per job. Compare that to your gross profit, not your revenue. And go in clear-eyed that you are renting: nothing stays yours when it ends.

Pay Per Appointment Marketing: Common Questions

What is pay per appointment marketing for contractors?

It is any arrangement where you pay a set price for each appointment delivered instead of a monthly fee or a price per lead. The vendor carries the cost of generating demand and getting someone to agree to a time, and you pay on the outcome. The model is real and it can work. What decides whether it works for you is not the price per appointment. It is the definition of the word appointment in your contract: who the person is, whether anyone else got the same appointment, what qualification was checked, and what happens when they do not show up.

How is pay per appointment different from pay per lead?

A lead is a name and a phone number. An appointment is a name, a phone number and a time on your calendar. The gap between those two things is where most of the money goes. On shared marketplaces you pay roughly $20 to $110 for a record and then do the calling, the qualifying and the booking yourself, and you pay whether you ever reach the person or not. Pay per appointment moves that work to the vendor. It also moves the incentive: a pay-per-lead vendor is paid for volume, a pay-per-booked vendor is paid for slots on a calendar, and a pay-per-held vendor is the only one paid for somebody actually sitting in front of you.

What does a pay per appointment vendor charge?

The vendors that rank for contractor appointment terms publish roughly $110 to $150 per appointment, depending on trade and market, and some bill against a prepaid balance and keep sending appointments until it runs out. That number on its own tells you almost nothing, because two vendors at $125 can be selling very different products. One may be selling a homeowner who agreed to a time and who is also meeting three other contractors that week. The other may be selling an exclusive, qualified appointment nobody else got. Same price, different thing.

What has to be defined in writing for an appointment to count?

Five things, and get all five before you sign. Who counts as a qualified homeowner, including whether they own the home and whether the project is in your price range. When the appointment has to be set for, so you are not billed for slots three weeks out. Whether the appointment is exclusive to you or shared with other contractors. What happens on a no-show, meaning a credit, a replacement, or nothing. And who you can talk to when an appointment is wrong. If a vendor will not put the no-show policy in the contract, that is your answer.

Does pay per appointment marketing work for deck builders?

It can, and the arithmetic is friendlier for decks than for most trades, because the job size is large enough to absorb a real cost per appointment. A builder selling $25,000 to $80,000 projects can pay well over $100 for an appointment and still be fine, as long as the appointment is exclusive and the homeowner is actually a buyer. Where it breaks down for deck builders is qualification and timing. A deck is a want purchase people plan for months, so a vendor paid per booked slot has every reason to book the homeowner who will say yes to a time, not the homeowner who is going to build.

What is the catch with pay per appointment?

You are paying someone per unit of something, so they will produce units. That is not dishonesty, it is incentive design. If the contract says booked, you will get bookings and the no-shows are your problem. If qualification is vague, you will get appointments that technically qualify. And you usually do not own anything at the end: not the ad account, not the creative, not the pixel history, not the list. Stop paying and the pipeline stops the same week with nothing left behind.

Is pay per appointment better than a retainer plus ad spend?

Neither is better in the abstract. They trade different risks. Pay per appointment caps your downside and costs you control and ownership. A retainer plus your own ad spend means you carry the risk of a bad month, and you own the account, the data and the audience that keeps working after the invoice stops. The honest test is what the arrangement owes you if it misses. Ask for the number and the clause instead of the pitch, and ask what of value stays yours when it ends.

How much do appointment setting services cost?

Published price points run from about $8.50 to $14.50 an hour for an offshore setter and $25 to $45 an hour for a US-based one (VA Masters), to $3,000 to $8,000 a month for an agency program, to roughly $50 to $500 per set appointment (Belkins). Pay per shown appointment costs more per unit, around $150 to $400 for small and mid-sized targets (Cleverly), because the vendor eats the no-shows. Most of those public numbers come from B2B firms, so ask any contractor-focused vendor whether their price counts a set, a shown or a sat appointment. We don't publish our price: you get the real number on a 30-minute strategy call.

What is pay per show?

Pay per show is a pricing model where you're billed only when the homeowner actually shows up for the appointment, not when they agree to a time. It costs more per appointment than pay per set, because the vendor carries the no-show risk instead of you. It still isn't the same as a sat appointment: a homeowner who shows up without their spouse counts as a show and rarely signs that day. So get the definition of a show in the contract before you compare prices.
