Contractor Video Marketing: 3 Mistakes That Kill Your Ads

Spencer Wright — Founder, MoreJobCalls
April 16, 2026 · Updated September 2026
Key Takeaway

The more professional a contractor's video looks, the faster homeowners scroll past it. Three mistakes cause most of the damage: content that sells instead of teaches, an opening that leads with your credentials instead of their problem, and production values that make the video read as an advertisement.

None of the three are budget problems. An iPhone, a real backyard and the homeowner's actual worry in the first three seconds beats a production crew almost every time.

MoreJobCalls.com is a marketing company for deck builders and other home-service contractors that books exclusive sales appointments onto the owner's calendar instead of selling shared leads. The numbers below come from client accounts I run.

The full version of this breakdown, from the More Job Calls YouTube channel.

Most contractors assume better content means better gear. Nicer camera, cleaner background, a polished intro. If you are going to put your business on camera, you want it to look good.

After about $10 million in ad spend across contractor accounts, here is the pattern: the more produced the video, the worse it performs.

Mistake 1: Selling Instead of Teaching

This one survives even after people fix their production. The video looks decent, the intro is fine, and then the rest of it is reasons to hire you. Special offer. Service list. Book today. Limited availability.

The homeowner watching does not trust you yet. The moment it feels like a pitch, their guard goes up, because every other contractor in their feed is doing the same thing. Everyone is selling. People have gone numb to it.

The contractors who get booked from content are not selling. They are answering the questions already running through a homeowner's head: what does this actually cost, how do I tell if someone is legitimate, what should I watch out for.

Jeff H., a 35-year veteran contractor in Michigan, used to treat winter as dead time. The instinct was to sell harder through it: discounts, promos, book-now messaging. None of it moved.

So the content flipped to teaching, built around something homeowners were already uneasy about: aging decks, structural safety, what happens if you wait until spring. Winter turned out to be a good time to deal with a safety issue before the rush. His January calendar went from crews stretching work to booked solid, and he now closes somewhere between 40 and 50% of cold traffic leads. His sales ability did not change. The content did the convincing before he arrived.

Every piece of content is a touchpoint, and trust accumulates across touchpoints. By the time you are at the kitchen table, it should not feel like a first meeting.

Mistake 2: Opening With Who You Are

Almost every contractor video starts the same way. "Hey, I'm Bob, I've been building decks for 22 years."

And people scroll, because nobody cares about Bob yet. They did not ask to hear from you. You appeared in their feed uninvited, and the only question in their mind is what this has to do with them. Answer that in the first three seconds or they are gone.

So open with what they are feeling instead. "If you've been putting off your deck project because you're worried about hiring the wrong contractor, this is for you." Now you have said the thing they have actually been thinking.

Chip P., a deck builder in Loomis, CA, hated his first ads. Thirty-three years in the trade, retired firefighter, handshake guy, and the scripts did not open with any of his credentials. He thought they made him sound like a used car salesman. They opened instead with the homeowner's frustration: the beauty contest of bids, the fear of picking wrong, the anxiety of spending $40,000 and getting burned.

In the ten months before that he had seen five or six deck bids total. In the first 21 days after the change he did $200,000 in new deck jobs, including two deals over $100,000 closed on a handshake. The message did not get louder. It got relevant.

Mistake 3: Making It Look Like an Ad

Contractors treat video like a billboard. Clean, polished, logo bumpers, graphics. On a social feed that is a signal to skip. The brain barely processes it. It does not matter how strong the offer is if nobody stops.

What stops a scroll is content that looks native: an iPhone video, a real project, real people, a backyard that looks like theirs.

Justin W., a deck and patio builder in San Antonio, sat around $2.5 million in revenue for a decade. He was paying a national agency more than $3,500 a month plus over $1,000 a week in ad spend for polished corporate creative, and paying $300 to $400 per lead.

That got scrapped for simple iPhone-style content: real backyards, real projects, real people. Cost per lead dropped to about $30 within the first week, roughly a 90% drop. Same market, same service, same budget. Only the creative changed. He added $1.5 million in revenue over the following six months, and later issued $1.7 million in quotes in a single week.

One More Thing: Ad Fatigue Is Real

This is not a one-time fix. A single good video will not carry a business. The contractors who stay booked keep shipping new angles, new jobs, new moments, because each new piece is another chance to interrupt a scroll.

  1. Film on the phone you already have, at a real job site.
  2. Open on the homeowner's problem, not your résumé.
  3. Teach one specific thing: a cost, a risk, a mistake to avoid.
  4. Keep the raw look. No logo intro, no graphics package.
  5. Shoot several while you are already on site, so you have rotation.

The settings side of the account matters too. The four settings that quietly waste an ad budget covers exclusions, lead forms, auto-optimization and retargeting, which are the other half of why accounts underperform.

The Honest Summary

Every video that sells instead of teaches is a missed touchpoint. Every video that opens with your name instead of their problem wastes the only three seconds you get. Every video that looks like an ad gets skipped by the exact homeowner who was already thinking about the project.

None of that is about budget. It is about whether the homeowner recognizes themselves in the first line.
